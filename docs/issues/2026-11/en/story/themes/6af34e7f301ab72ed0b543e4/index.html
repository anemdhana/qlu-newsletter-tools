Puranic Background of the Dhumravarna Story

The speaker introduces how the Dhumravarna avatar is told in the Mudgala Purana in the form of dialogues. Through names such as Saunaka, Suta Maharshi, Daksha, and Mudgala Maharshi, the tradition by which the story passed from one person to another is recalled. Referring to the conversation that took place between Shiva and Vishnu, it is explained that the Ganapati avatar is not merely a story of the destruction of a demon, but a tattva story that reveals the nature of ahankara.

The speaker’s method is to connect the wonderful events in the story with present-day life when listening to them. Through the Purana saying that even the gods can develop ahankara, the message is brought forward that no one should think they are completely beyond this tendency.

From the Subtle Ahankara of the Sun to the Birth of Ahankarasura

The story proceeds that in the management of creation Brahma entrusts a part of his responsibilities to the Sun. Along with that responsibility a slight feeling of “I alone am doing it” arises in the Sun. Bhanumurti explains the Puranic episode that from the sneeze that came at that time Ahankarasura was born. He sees the asura who began small quickly becoming big as an indication that ahankara begins as a small thought and can strengthen rapidly.

When we receive a responsibility, success, or recognition we can accept it as service; or we can turn it into self-importance with “this became possible only because of me.” The difference between these two viewpoints is subtle, yet the result is large, he says. Recognizing the birth of ahankara is the first step in working with it.

Guidance of Shukracharya and the Boon of Ganesha

Shukracharya stands as guru to Ahankarasura and suggests that he perform upasana of Ganesha. After long tapas Ganesha becomes pleased, and Ahankarasura asks for the boon that death should not come through beings covered by maya or possessing blood and flesh. The determination he showed in this stage of acquiring power is remarkable; yet the direction in which the purpose of that power turned after obtaining it is a crucial turning point in the story.

In this context the speaker explains that tapas, discipline, and power do not by themselves produce humility. Even if capacity increases through sadhana, without inner inquiry that capacity itself can become the basis for a sense of domination. Therefore he suggests that we should frequently look at what our intention is.

Vishayapriya City, Mamata, Garva, and Shreshtha

After obtaining the boon the story says that Ahankarasura established a city called Vishayapriya, married Mamata, and obtained sons named Garva and Shreshtha. Bhanumurti specially analyzes these names. He interprets Vishayapriya as attraction toward sense objects; Mamata as the insistence of “mine”; Garva as self-praise; and Shreshtha as the feeling “there is no one greater than me.”

He explains that these tendencies do not exist one by one, but mutually strengthen one another like links in a chain. When the healthy state of “I know something” changes into the closed state of “I alone know everything,” it becomes difficult to listen to others’ words. The role of companions who encourage adharma is also connected with this context in the story.

Domination over the Three Worlds — Expansion of Ahankara

Ahankarasura’s desires do not stop with his city. In the attempt to gain authority over the three worlds he subjects the gods and other beings to fear. Because of the effect of Ganesha’s boon it is not possible to confront him, and the gods discuss among themselves. Finally they decide that they must pray to the same Ganesha who gave the boon.

Bhanumurti reminds us that the ahankara within us also does not stop with one victory. It expands in forms such as wanting everyone to accept our opinion, looking down on others, and trying to control even relationships. This story shows that even while outer success increases, inner peace may decrease.

Appearance of Dhumravarna and Narada’s Peace Message

The story proceeds that Ganesha responds to the gods’ prayer and appears in the form of Dhumravarna. Even before the war he sends Narada as messenger and gives the message that Ahankarasura should stop his evil deeds and come for peace. Even though warning signs appear to Ahankarasura, with pride in his power he rejects that opportunity.

The speaker compares this episode with the warnings that come in our life. Sometimes others’ words, a failure, or a problem in a relationship may remind us to correct our behavior. If we can listen to those indications immediately we can prevent a larger conflict. However, when ahankara is strong, even the advice we receive may appear as an insult.

War, Loss, and Returning to the Guru

After rejecting peace the war begins. Before Dhumravarna’s power Ahankarasura’s army cannot stand; his sons also perish. With all his supports collapsing, the frightened Ahankarasura returns to Shukracharya. He admits that he did not heed the indication that had come earlier and that he does not know what to do now.

Then the guru tells him that there is no other way except sharanagati. Here sharanagati is not merely accepting defeat; it is the state of being able to see the loss that occurred because of one’s ahankara. The speaker explains that the main transformation in the story is stopping the covering up of one’s mistake and becoming ready for change.

Ahankara Melting in the Presence of Ganesha

With the darshana of Ganesha the pride in Ahankarasura decreases, and he praises him and seeks refuge. Even though Dhumravarna forgives him, he reminds him of the harm done in the past and warns him not to increase that tendency henceforth. In this episode priority is given to transformation rather than punishment.

Knowing that there is ahankara in us is one stage; accepting the pain caused to others because of it is a deeper stage. Bhanumurti says, based on this story, that true change appears only through asking for forgiveness, listening, and changing behavior.

Smoke, Jnana Agni, Jnana Khadga — Symbols of Dhumravarna

Dhumra means smoke. Just as there is fire behind smoke, the speaker symbolically explains that jnana agni is needed behind inner purification. Our old habits and concepts covered by maya come into the light through knowledge. It is suggested that the form of Dhumravarna should be seen not merely as a fearsome form, but as the power of transformation that removes ahankara.

In the context of Kaliyuga he also mentions symbols such as the blue horse and the jnana khadga. That sword is explained as a symbol of knowledge that pierces ignorance and maya, not for conquering outsiders. These explanations are the tattvarthas given by the speaker; the sense appears in his words that there is no need to read them only as physical events.

Meditation, Swadhyaya, and the Question “Who Am I?”

Bhanumurti says that merely listening to the story is not enough to reduce ahankara. While doing meditation one should continue swadhyaya; one should observe how our reactions, judgments, and comparisons are changing. He warns that deciding by comparing experiences arising in meditation with others — “I got more” or “I didn’t get it” — can lead to another kind of ahankara.

He suggests that questions such as “Who am I?” and “What is this situation teaching me?” should be made part of life inquiry. Seeing Ganesha first in the temple is also explained as a symbol of leaving ahankara there and entering within. The essence of the story: recognizing the ahankara within us and living with humility is more important than conquering Ahankarasura outside.
