Topic: Let Us Shape Life More Beautifully with Love and Understanding
Original Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NSNJf76rWeU

1. Speaking Peacefully: Spiritual Practice in Daily Life

In this meeting, the second day of the Speak Peace series, Devi introduces nonviolent conversation not merely as a communication technique but as a practice that makes life more beautiful. Recalling the feelings that Kiran explained in the previous class, she brings forward the question of how the theory we have learned should come into our behavior. The real test is what we are doing when others’ words do not please us, when criticism is faced, and when anger arises in the mind.

It may be easy for us to talk about peace. But maintaining the same peace in a difficult conversation requires much practice. The main direction Devi explains is that change should come in daily life through that practice; it should be useful to us and to the other person in every context. She connects this idea with the Nonviolent Communication method developed by Marshall Rosenberg.

2. The Value of the Words Spoken by Patriji

During meditation Devi recalls the words of Brahmarshi Pitamaha Patriji. She explains that he used to say that just as our face reveals the state in the mind, our speech expresses the level of our inner self. Joy or pain may be visible on a person’s face; however, how that person is speaking reveals more clearly their inner understanding, culture, and manner of responding.

What is important in this teaching is not only speaking sweetly outwardly. What is the intention behind the speech? Is it to blame, to win, to wound, or truly to understand? If the impulse in the mind comes out as it is, the speech also carries the same impulse. Therefore if we want to change the speech we must first observe the inner state that is its source.

3. Clear Speech Is Obtained Only When the Mind Is Stopped

Attributing it to Pothuluri Veerabrahmendra Swami, Devi mentions the idea of a verse: speech spoken without stopping the mind has no proper result; if the mind is stopped clear speech is obtained. Some words in the verse are not clear in the automatic captions; therefore here we are providing only the commentary on the meaning she gave in the discourse, without reprinting the verse as it is.

Stopping the mind does not mean only stopping words from coming. It means setting aside for some time the imaginations, judgments, analyses, and accusations that form immediately about the other person. Creating space to know what is happening in us and what is happening in the other person. Devi explains that when done so, in place of the speech that came with impulse, speech with truth, clarity, and compassion may come.

In this context the clear speech she speaks of does not have the meaning of mere eloquence. It is conversation that is honest and that respects the humanity of others. It is difficult to choose the right words without recognizing the confusion in the mind; that is why inward examination is needed before peaceful conversation.

4. The Ornament That Remains Is the Ornament of Speech

Devi also recalls a shloka from Bhartrihari’s Subhashitas that tells the glory of speech. Ornaments, pearl necklaces, fragrant substances, and beautiful decorations give external beauty to a person. However, they are things that decline with time. She explains the shloka as saying that speech with culture is the ornament that remains for a person.

This idea is a strong support for her main teaching. Good speech does not mean speech said to please others. There should be truth, compassion, and respect in it. External decoration may attract a person for a moment; but the gentleness and trustworthiness in speech show long-term influence in relationships. This vision that connects a person’s beauty with his speech makes known why the quality of words is important in NVC practice.

5. From Traditional Knowledge to Marshall Rosenberg

Devi says that she first came to know Marshall Rosenberg’s teachings through the previous NVC series in Jnanodayam. She shares that from then she has been listening to his videos frequently, and that as she listens her respect for his effort is increasing. What attracted her in this method is that it does not remain merely as a great theory; it shows a practical path useful to every person every day.

She reminds us that in traditional spiritual teachings too there are many indications about speech, compassion, and self-inquiry. She sees as the speciality in Rosenberg’s effort the turning of that knowledge into a method useful for the conflicts in life. This comparison is Devi’s personal spiritual commentary; it should be remembered that she is seeing the life journeys of different gurus with the connection she has experienced.

6. Why Compassion? Why Violence? — An Inquiry

According to Devi’s explanation, from a young age Rosenberg asked himself questions about human behavior. Why do some remain with compassion even in the most difficult situations? Why do others go toward violence? What is the cause of violent behavior? Why are all people not able to remain with compassion? Devi says that for the answer to these questions he went toward psychology and continued higher studies.

She explains that thinking that his inquiry was not completed with academic study alone, he also directed his attention toward other texts, thought traditions, and people’s experiences. She compares this journey with the questioning nature of the Buddha. This comparison with the Buddha’s life story is not to prove historical equality; it was used to indicate the inquiry of wanting to know what is the root of human suffering.

7. Taking the Learned Knowledge Among People

Devi specially says that Rosenberg did not limit his understanding only to books. She explains that he worked in various environments such as educational institutions, offices, prisons, and areas where conflicts are occurring. She considers that this work was more important to him than the opportunities of earning through his profession. She compares this service viewpoint with the manner in which Brahmarshi Pitamaha Patriji went among the people and spread meditation.

Especially she recalls the contexts in which he tried for conversation between groups that were in intense enmity with each other. In one example she explains that there was conflict so intense that loss of life occurred between two groups, and that even in such situations he tried for mediation. She says that even if those in the conflict left the meeting, he worked with patience to bring them back to a state of talking. The numbers and specific event details in this example have not been independently verified; here we are reflecting only the explanation in the discourse.

8. What Is the Feeling? What Is the Judgment? — The First Inner Churning

Devi shares honestly that when she began NVC practice it felt very difficult. When someone says something it is easy for anger to come. But if one stops immediately and asks ‘What is the feeling arising in me now?’ the answer may not be found. She says that in the beginning the difference of whether what we are saying is a feeling or an analysis or judgment about the other person may not be known.

After recognizing that feeling another question is faced: ‘What is the unmet need behind this feeling?’ There too the mind may feel like a blank paper, she says. Even though words are ready to throw out at the moment anger comes, words are not found so easily to recognize our need. That is why she compares this practice with the churning of the ocean of milk. When looking inward what is first faced may be discomfort, confusion, impulse; only after crossing them does clearer understanding become possible.

9. Is Our Natural Nature Compassion?

While explaining Rosenberg’s teaching, Devi says that the natural nature of a human being is to give with love, to receive, and to live in mutual connection. However, a strong question arises here: if human nature is compassion, why is so much violence visible in the world? She explains in the discourse the context in which someone asked this question to Rosenberg.

As the answer he gave, the meaning Devi states is that the language, training, and social habits we have learned for generations have taken us away from natural compassion. She attributes to him that such habits have formed for thousands of years. This is a philosophical explanation in the discourse; the mentioned time period should not be taken as a confirmed historical measure. The important message, however, is clear: merely because a violent response has become well habituated to us does not mean it is unchangeable.

10. From Words That Teach Retaliation Toward New Conversation

Devi mentions the examples of elders encouraging when children quarrel and come home: ‘Why didn’t you hit back?’, ‘You should also say to those who said to you!’ From childhood the idea that counter-attacking itself is strength may have formed in us. Speaking that way may feel like having won, like having maintained our honor. If the same response continues for generations, it is no wonder that it feels natural to us.

However, she suggests that blaming parents or previous generations for these habits is not the solution. They also learned the language and manner of response that came to them. Now we can recognize that pattern and change it. Part of that change is, when anger comes, not immediately seeing the other person as an enemy, but observing the feeling and need within us and trying to understand their situation as well.

11. The Joy of Speaking Back Immediately — The Satisfaction After Understanding

Explaining her personal experience, Devi says that from childhood it was her nature to give an answer immediately if anyone said anything. She recalls that even if she did not go near anyone, if anyone came near her she would not remain quiet. She explains that when she spoke back immediately it felt good for that moment, but in her practice she observed that it was only a superficial, temporary satisfaction.

Differently from that, she says that deeper inner satisfaction arose when, instead of responding immediately after hearing a word, she stopped, saw what was happening in herself, understood the other person, and then spoke nonviolently. The change she speaks of here is not remaining weak and silent. It is regaining the freedom to respond. Going toward a state in which understanding decides our speech, not impulse.

Devi says that she questioned herself as to how it is if we who are meditating also do not practice this. She obtained in that the inspiration that when Rosenberg was able to work among ordinary people and amid conflicts, those who are doing meditation practice can pay more attention to changing their conversation. Only when the experience of peace is reflected in our words and relationships does that practice seem to have come into life.
