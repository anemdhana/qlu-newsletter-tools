The Inner Meaning of Cause and Symbols — Toward New Birth in Fresh Consciousness

Worldly responsibilities and the longing for liberation need not be enemies. We can meet our duties without becoming imprisoned by them. We can understand desires rather than merely suppress them, notice when the mind magnifies suffering, and steadily examine the vasanas and samskaras that shape our reactions. The gold mine of inner potential is not elsewhere. The invitation of this fourth day is to discover it through awareness, practice, and the surrender of ego.

Cause, Symbolism, and Spiritual Rebirth

On the fourth day of Devi Navratri, Dr. Newton explores the story of Rambha and Karambha as a way to understand two impulses within us: the desire to engage with worldly life and the longing for liberation. Through stories about the mind, impressions, and inner freedom, he invites listeners to approach the festival as a living spiritual practice rather than merely a celebration.

Celebrating Navratri Through Understanding

Good morning, my dear friends! As we celebrate Devi Navratri, let us not stop at remembering the names and forms of Jagannmata, Adi Shakti, and Para Shakti. Let us try to understand and experience the spiritual insight represented by each day.

Every event has a cause. Our birth has a cause; the stories of the devas and asuras also have contexts and purposes. Unless we examine the background of a story, its deeper teaching can escape us. The sages conveyed practical insights through festivals and symbolic narratives. A festival becomes a spiritual celebration when its teachings begin to change how we live.

Just as a child spends nine months in the mother's womb before being born, these nine nights can symbolize a new inner birth: a growing recognition of the divine energy within us. Let us approach the origin of Mahishasura through that lens.

Rambha and Karambha — Two Longings Within Us

The story speaks of two brothers, Rambha and Karambha. In this lecture, their austerities are interpreted as expressions of two inner tendencies.

Rambha represents worldly desire. We want possessions, success, recognition, and circumstances to unfold according to our expectations. When life does not obey our wishes, disappointment, anger, or sorrow can arise. Our attention remains entangled in external events.

Rambha's austerity in water is read symbolically: water suggests Maya and the depths of emotion. Just as the surface of the sea conceals what lies beneath, the visible world can seem to be the whole of reality. We may blame others for our suffering without examining the habitual responses within us.

Karambha represents the determination to practise without being lost in Maya. His austerity amid the five fires and beneath a banyan tree is related to the challenge posed by the five senses. Sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch continually draw our attention outward. The task is to discover inward steadiness amid those attractions.

Do not regard these brothers only as people in an ancient story. Both tendencies live in us: the wish to fulfil our responsibilities and desires, and the longing to know our true nature. We need not abandon our work or families; neither should we forget the deeper purpose of our lives.

Indra and the Crocodile — When the Mind Interrupts Practice

In the narrative, Indra appears in the form of a crocodile and pulls the ascetic into the water. Dr. Newton interprets Indra, traditionally associated with the senses, as a symbol of the mind.

Observe what happens when you sit to meditate. Memories arise. Someone's words return. Worries about tomorrow appear. Even when you intend to rest your attention on the breath, the mind repeatedly draws you outward. The crocodile becomes an image of this pull.

The real struggle is not with other people but with our own unexamined reactions, habits, and restless thoughts. Another episode speaks of offering the head into fire. This is explained as the symbolic surrender of the mind and ego, not as an act of physical harm to imitate.

Clarity of Purpose — Samsara and Nirvana

My dear friends, why have we come into this life? What is it that we truly wish to accomplish? Education, livelihood, family, and earning a living all matter. But the activities necessary for survival should not automatically become the whole purpose of our existence.

Adults sometimes decide what their children must become, projecting their own unrealized ambitions onto them. Inner observation helps us recognize which aspirations are truly ours.

Rambha and Karambha illuminate the tension between Samsara, with its practical duties and pleasures, and Nirvana, the longing for freedom from suffering and for Self-knowledge. Rejecting family life does not by itself create spiritual maturity. Nor does endless pursuit of external success bring lasting freedom. The teaching is to live responsibly while cultivating inward independence.

Putra and Dvija — The Meaning of a New Birth

The image of surrendering the head to fire points toward relinquishing ego and turning toward the Self. In the lecture, the word putra is given a symbolic interpretation linked to freedom from the cycle of birth and death. Dvija, or 'twice-born', is explored as a second birth through knowledge.

These are spiritual interpretations offered in the lecture, not claims that they are the sole or standard linguistic meanings of these terms.

A calendar date changing does not make our life new if our old habits remain intact. A spiritual new birth begins when the way we see ourselves and respond to life begins to change.

The Halwa Example — Suppression Is Not Freedom

Imagine someone who loves halwa and announces, 'I have renounced it; I will never eat it again.' Yet as soon as the person sits for meditation, thoughts of halwa return. The desire rejected outwardly remains active inwardly.

Suppressing a desire is different from becoming free of it. Forceful rejection may leave the attraction untouched. Instead, the practitioner must observe desire and understand how the mind sustains it.

The lecture also evokes the image of a drop of water resting on a lotus leaf without clinging to it. In the same way, we can participate in family, work, and society without losing our inner freedom through attachment.

Ramana Maharshi's Story — Who Steals Our Peace?

Dr. Newton recalls a story about thieves entering Ramanasramam. While some disciples reacted angrily, Ramana Maharshi remained calm. The incident is used to point toward the mind as the thief that can steal our happiness.

Someone says something hurtful. The event itself may last a moment, but the mind repeats it for hours: 'Why did they speak that way? Why did they insult me?' The inner story grows larger than the original event.

This does not mean tolerating injustice or refusing necessary action. We can respond appropriately while observing how anger, resentment, and the wish for retaliation arise within us.

A question for practice: Is my peace being disturbed by the event itself, or by the story my mind keeps telling about it?

Vasanas and Samskaras — The Soiled Cloth

Consider a dirty cloth being washed. The visible dirt may disappear after the first wash, yet an unpleasant smell remains. Further washing is needed before the cloth is truly clean.

The story illustrates vasanas and samskaras, the tendencies and impressions that continue to influence our responses. We may believe anger has disappeared, only to find it returning under pressure. We may think pride has dissolved, yet criticism still hurts.

A single meditation session does not necessarily erase longstanding tendencies. Continuous awareness, practice, and inner purification are needed. We learn to recognize the influence of tamasic and rajasic habits rather than assume they have vanished.

The Gold Mine in Our Own Backyard

A poor man is told by a wise person that a treasure lies buried in his own backyard. He does not believe it and lives without discovering it. Someone else eventually takes the advice, digs, and finds the treasure.

The hidden gold represents the wisdom and spiritual potential already within us. We may search everywhere outside while neglecting the inward exploration urged by the teachers. Hearing about the treasure is not enough: we must undertake the practice that reveals it.

The Mosquito and the Elephant — A Wider Sense of Self

A mosquito rests on an elephant and later apologizes for any inconvenience it may have caused. The elephant replies that it had not even noticed the mosquito's presence.

The mosquito represents a narrow self-preoccupation; the elephant suggests the vastness and steadiness of the Self. Small incidents can seem enormous when we identify completely with our personal pride. A wider understanding of who we are can help us meet them without being shaken by everything that happens.

Devi Samarpana — The Spirit of Surrender

The lecture closes by returning to Devi Samarpana. Dr. Newton demonstrates a practice involving a Lakshmi-mudra orientation, attention to the left side, breathing through the left nostril while gently closing the right, bowing in a gesture of reverence, and inward remembrance of the bija syllable Shreem (శ్రీం). Nine rounds are mentioned.

The central meaning is not forceful control of breathing, but the surrender of ego. Remembering Jagannmata, Adi Shakti, and Para Shakti becomes an invitation to quieten the mind and cultivate humility.

Bringing the Teaching into Daily Life

Worldly responsibilities and the longing for liberation need not be enemies. We can meet our duties without becoming imprisoned by them. We can understand desires rather than merely suppress them, notice when the mind magnifies suffering, and steadily examine the vasanas and samskaras that shape our reactions. The gold mine of inner potential is not elsewhere. The invitation of this fourth day is to discover it through awareness, practice, and the surrender of ego.
