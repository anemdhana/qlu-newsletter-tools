The First Step in Receiving Wisdom — Becoming an Empty Vessel to Experience Shakti and Consciousness

Devi Navratri is not merely a festival to be observed. It is an opportunity to recognise the energy dormant within us, direct that energy towards inner practice, and learn to live every moment in the joy of the Self. Today, let us explore the relationship between Shakti and consciousness, the distinction between enjoyment and celebration, surrender to the Divine Mother, total acceptance, and the breath-and-bowing practice of Devi Samarpana.

Speaker: Brahmarshi Chakravarthy Dr. Newton Kondaveti
Topic: Devi Navratri — Shakti, Consciousness, Surrender, and Total Acceptance
Original Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dmLVPT3065I

01. Shakti and Consciousness — The Two Fundamental Principles of Creation

Wonderful friends! During Devi Navratri, we must understand the deeper meaning of these nine days. Only then can we truly celebrate them.

Throughout creation, we recognise two fundamental principles: Shakti and Chaitanya — energy and consciousness. We call Shakti by many names: Parvati Mata, Durga Devi, Adi Shakti, Para Shakti, and Parameshwari. We call consciousness Shiva, Ishwara, Sarveshwara, Sarvantaryami, and Paramatma. The names may differ, but these are the essential principles we need to understand.

Without understanding Shakti, it is difficult to understand consciousness fully. Therefore, let us dedicate these nine days not merely to learning about Shakti, but to experiencing that energy within ourselves.

Central insight: We speak of energy and consciousness using different names and forms. The purpose of spiritual practice is to experience their connection.

02. Enjoyment Is Different from Celebration

Observe the difference between enjoyment and celebration. I want something. When I get it, I feel happy. I want ice cream; when I eat it, I say I enjoyed it. I want to see a film; after watching it, I say I enjoyed it. First there is the desire: “I want.” Enjoyment follows when that desire is fulfilled.

Celebration is different. Celebration does not depend on the condition that I must first obtain something. It is a joy that arises from within, regardless of how external circumstances are arranged. To live every moment wonderfully, in the happiness arising from the Self, is celebration.

These nine days are therefore not simply for enjoyment. They are for celebration. A festival is not limited to external entertainment. The real celebration is to experience Para Shakti within and live with that awareness.

If you are facing problems, feel stuck at some stage of life, or cannot yet find answers, dedicate these days to spiritual practice. Spiritual tradition regards this period as especially significant for Shakti sadhana.

03. From Outward Activity to the Inward Journey

Energy can be expressed outwardly or directed inward. In this discourse, outward expression is associated symbolically with the masculine principle, and inward movement with the feminine principle. These are symbolic categories used for spiritual practice, not fixed descriptions of women and men. Everyone can practise turning inward.

Notice how much of our energy is scattered outward: through speech, thoughts, activity, and unnecessary attractions. For these nine days, let us change that direction.

Instead of scattering energy outside, let us gather it within ourselves and use it for spiritual practice. There are many mysteries and profound insights within us. We need energy to discover and experience them.

The central purpose of these nine days is to experience the Para Shakti described as lying dormant within us.

Practice principle: Stop wasting energy through needless outward distraction. Redirect attention and energy inward.

04. The Three Shaktis — Meditation, Service, and Self-Knowledge

We worship Para Shakti in many forms: Saraswati Mata, Lakshmi Mata, Durga Mata, and Kali Mata. Though their names and forms differ, we are honouring the same Divine Energy.

Let us remember three essential spiritual practices through these forms:

  • Durga/Kali Shakti — Meditation: Turning inward and experiencing the energy within.
  • Lakshmi Shakti — Service: Using our energy for the wellbeing of others.
  • Saraswati Shakti — Self-knowledge: Understanding our true nature.

Dhyana, Seva, and Atma Jnana — meditation, service, and Self-knowledge — form the practice of the three Shaktis. These three can help us progress when we feel spiritually stuck.

Tradition also speaks of bija aksharas, seed syllables associated with forms of Shakti. Because the automatic captions are unclear about the exact correspondences in this passage, this edition does not supply a definitive mapping.

05. Devi Samarpana — Seeing the Mother in Everyone

Let us spend these nine days in Devi Samarpana, the spirit of surrender to the Divine Mother. Remain connected with yourself in everything you do. Learn to see Jaganmata in everyone.

When you look into a mirror, see the Mother within yourself. When you meet another person, recognise that same Shakti there. Whether the person is a woman or a man, cultivate the feeling that Para Shakti is manifesting through that individual.

This is not merely a matter of calling everyone “Amma.” Our way of seeing must change. Instead of seeing people only through their names, appearances, and social roles, let us learn to see them as expressions of that energy. Let us extend this feeling to trees, animals, nature, and the five great elements.

06. The Symbol of Shiva and Shakti

Shiva symbolises consciousness. Tradition says that without Shakti, even Shiva would be like shava — an inert body. The deeper meaning is that without energy, there is no manifestation. That is why we must first understand Shakti.

In a story associated with the fierce form of the Divine Mother, she destroys the asuras and manifests overwhelming energy. Shiva is said to lie beneath her feet to bring that force to awareness. When her foot touches his heart, awareness arises.

Let us understand this as a symbolic teaching about the relationship between energy and consciousness, rather than only as an outward event.

07. Feeling the Presence of the Divine Mother

Recall the precious moments you spent with your parents. Even when they are not physically before you, the bond you formed allows you to feel their presence in your heart. Their companionship lives in your memories.

In the same way, during these nine days let us feel the presence of Adi Shakti. Just as we have physical parents, we may regard Shakti as our spiritual mother and consciousness as our spiritual father.

The sages and yogis gave many names and forms to the formless Divine Energy. Those forms help us establish a relationship. But we should not stop at the form. At first, we worship the Mother as though she were separate from us. As practice deepens, we move towards the experience that we are not separate from that energy.

Remember: A form can help us establish a connection; the deeper purpose of practice is to experience the presence of Shakti.

08. Lakshmi Mudra — A Symbol of Inwardness

During these nine days, even our sitting posture can serve as a reminder. In this discourse, placing the left leg over the right and the left hand over the right is explained as a symbolic expression of Lakshmi Mudra.

The intention is to give priority to the Shakti principle and remember not to scatter our energy outward.

We may have many spiritual aspirations: to understand the Ajna chakra, experience Sahasrara, attain Self-knowledge, or release the burden of karma. But wishing alone is not enough. We must practise. Otherwise, as the proverb suggests, we remain exactly where we were. Spiritual progress requires us to use our energy purposefully.

09. Total Acceptance

Another important practice for these nine days is Total Acceptance.

Some things in life please us; others do not. We may like sweetness and dislike the bitterness of bitter gourd. People, too, have different temperaments. Some speak sweetly; others may feel difficult to us.

Let us learn to accept people as they are. Reduce the expectations that someone must change in a particular way to suit us. Accept yourself as you are, too.

Only when we recognise and accept our present state can we move to another state. Acceptance does not mean abandoning growth. It means ceasing to reject the reality from which growth must begin.

Write down the circumstances you struggle to accept. Which people do you continually expect to change? What do you reject about yourself?

Practise acceptance from morning until night. As that practice becomes natural, life itself can become a continuous celebration.

10. Dasara — Living Joyfully

Playing with the sound of the word Dasara, the lecture reminds us to live with joy and lightness. The important point is not a linguistic derivation but the invitation to celebrate every moment.

Celebration does not require external objects. Do not assume happiness can come only when a desire is satisfied. We are more than the physical body. Let us discover the joy arising from the Self.

11. Red and White — Symbols in Devi Worship

Red is prominent in worship of the Divine Mother: in kumkum, clothing, and decorations. In this discourse, red represents dynamic, manifest energy. White and red are also used as symbolic colours.

Do not see these merely as decorations. Our sages embedded teachings in stories, festivals, and customs. A festival has two purposes: to celebrate and to learn and practise spiritual wisdom.

12. Chandra Nadi, Surya Nadi, and Sushumna

Breathing through the two nostrils does not always feel identical; airflow may seem stronger on one side at a given moment. Yogic tradition associates the left nostril with Chandra Nadi and the right with Surya Nadi.

Here, Chandra Nadi is associated with Shakti and inwardness, while Surya Nadi is associated with outward activity. The feeling of balanced breathing during meditation is explained symbolically through Sushumna Nadi and the upward movement of energy. These are explanations within yogic tradition, not established anatomical descriptions.

Let us now connect these ideas with the practice of Devi Samarpana.

13. Bowing the Head — Surrendering the Ego

In our culture, we bow before elders, parents, and deities. In some temples, entrances become smaller as we approach the inner shrine, prompting us to lower our heads. We can understand this as a symbol.

We use expressions such as “it has gone to his head” when someone becomes proud of wealth, status, or power. To bow the head can symbolise letting go of ego.

We honour our parents, through whom we entered physical life. In the same spirit, let us bow with surrender before Jaganmata, the Shakti understood as the source of creation.

14. The Devi Samarpana Breath-and-Bowing Practice

Let us learn the practice introduced today. Its purpose is not to bend the body mechanically. The essential feeling is: “I offer my ego, my problems, and my aspirations at the feet of the Divine Mother.”

Sequence demonstrated in the discourse

  1. Sit comfortably. If using a chair, choose a position appropriate for your body. You may remember the symbolic intention of Lakshmi Mudra.
  2. The discourse demonstrates Vishnu Mudra with the right hand and gently closing the right nostril.
  3. It demonstrates taking the breath in through the left nostril in nine small successive stages. This is a specific pranayama technique, not ordinary relaxed breathing.
  4. The hands are then brought into a gesture of salutation. While breathing out, the head is lowered slowly with the feeling of Devi Samarpana.
  5. In the bowed position, the practitioner is invited to count to nine or inwardly repeat the bija syllable “Shreem”, without chanting it aloud.
  6. Return upright slowly. The discourse demonstrates nine cycles and recommends the practice in the morning and, when possible, in the evening.

Important safety note: The demonstration also includes kumbhaka (breath retention), bahya kumbhaka (holding after exhalation), and bending the head. These are not appropriate for everyone. People with neck or spinal problems, dizziness, respiratory or cardiac conditions should not attempt breath-retention versions without suitable professional guidance. The feeling of surrender can also be practised while sitting comfortably and breathing naturally.

The intention during the nine cycles

Throughout the demonstration, the central reminder is to bow with the inner feeling of “Sharana, Sharana” — I surrender. Offer the problems you cannot resolve, the obstacles you find difficult to cross, and your aspirations for spiritual growth to the Divine Mother.

The final cycle is performed independently, without spoken guidance, in full awareness. The intention of surrender matters more than the number alone.

15. Remembering the Mother's Presence Throughout the Day

After morning meditation, begin the day in the spirit of surrender. Continue to remember the Mother's presence in everyone you meet.

The invitation for these nine days is to deepen our connection with the Mother principle. This does not diminish Shiva or consciousness; the focus of this particular practice is first to establish a connection with Shakti.

The discourse expresses the hope that sincere practice may bring us to a new spiritual state by Vijayadashami. Rather than measuring progress by extraordinary external events, we can observe changes in inwardness, acceptance, surrender, and joy.

16. Daily Practice — The Essential Reminders

  • Devi Samarpana: Offer the ego to the Shakti within.
  • Presence: Remember Jaganmata within yourself and others.
  • Lakshmi Mudra as a symbol: Turn from outward distraction towards inward awareness.
  • Total Acceptance: Accept people, situations, and your present state.
  • Celebration: Live joyfully without waiting for every desire to be fulfilled.
  • Meditation, Service, and Self-knowledge: Bring the three Shaktis into daily practice.

17. Participants' Experiences and Questions

Krishnakumari — Meditation, Daily Life, and a Terrace Garden

Krishnakumari from Vizianagaram shared her experience. She said she had experienced depression about three years earlier and had taken medication for a period. Through Bhanu, she learned about meditation and began attending Jnanodayam classes. With Harika's guidance, she practised grounding meditation and other exercises, gradually becoming more active in daily life.

She also reported that she had stopped taking medication. This is a participant's personal account, not a recommendation for anyone to discontinue treatment for depression.

The response appreciated her terrace garden and the time she spent among flowering plants, encouraging her connection with nature as an expression of Jaganmata. The speaker also mentioned plans, at the time of the lecture, to visit Rajahmundry, Visakhapatnam, and Vizianagaram for programmes in November.

Vikram — Acceptance, Emotional Burdens, and Medical Decisions

Vikram said that following a family constellation session, a family difficulty had begun to change. He remembered the teaching “What is in the way is the way.” He also described years of inflammation and shoulder pain, and said that laser surgery had recently been suggested for haemorrhoids. He wondered whether carrying too much responsibility for others' emotions contributed to his difficulties, and asked whether he should proceed with surgery or continue meditation.

The response emphasised that knowledge bears fruit when put into practice. It encouraged him to notice burdens and limiting beliefs, and to use meditation to examine emotions he may be carrying. The speaker also proposed a spiritual interpretation connecting physical conditions with particular emotional conflicts, including an identity conflict in relation to haemorrhoids.

Medical clarification: There is no established medical basis for assigning haemorrhoids, inflammation, or shoulder pain to a specific emotional conflict. This should be understood as a spiritual interpretation expressed during the lecture, not a medical diagnosis. Decisions about surgery should be made with the treating clinician; meditation is not a substitute for medical care.

The response then encouraged inner questioning and listening for clarity during meditation. Self-reflection may support a decision, but it cannot replace clinical evidence when deciding on medical treatment.

Naresh — The Eighteen Shakti Peethas

Naresh said that he had meditated for seven years without much regularity and had recently started attending Jnanodayam more consistently. He had listened to the Shwasa Mahavidya classes and learned the method of offering pranam to Jaganmata. He asked for an explanation of the Ashtadasha Shakti Peethas — the eighteen Shakti Peethas.

The response described them as ancient places of spiritual significance, associated in the tradition with generations of practice. The speaker referred to meditating at such places and experiencing their atmosphere. Because the topic was extensive, he proposed discussing the Shakti Peethas in greater detail during one of the remaining Navratri sessions. The eighteen sites were not explained individually in this session.

18. Mahishasuramardini Stotram — A Meditative Conclusion

After the questions and shared experiences, the speaker requested a rendition of the Mahishasuramardini Stotram. Participants were invited to listen in a meditative state, with eyes closed if they wished. The stotram was then sung.

The Central Teaching of Day 2

Understand Shakti. Turn energy that is scattered outward towards the inner journey. Practise meditation, service, and Self-knowledge. Recognise Jaganmata in everyone. Reduce expectations and accept life fully. Surrender the ego, and live every moment as a celebration.
