From the Background of Jalandhara to the Story of Kamasura

Before introducing the Vikata avatar, Harika Suri explains the Puranic background related to Jalandhara. Saying how one event led to another, she introduces the causal sequence that existed before the birth of Kamasura. She suggests that the Puranic story should be seen not as a list of separate characters, but as the sequence of how tendencies influence one another.

As soon as the name Kamasura is heard the topic of desire comes forward. However, the speaker explains that desire is not wrong merely because it exists; the problem begins when desire starts to gain mastery over us. She examines every stage in the story with this question: Are we driving the desire, or is the desire driving us?

From Desire to Endless Trishna

It is natural for a person to have needs. Harika Suri recognizes desires such as earning for livelihood, taking care of the family, and striving for good opportunities as ordinary parts of life. However, she explains that if, as soon as one goal is reached, the restlessness that another goal is necessarily needed begins, then contentment is postponed forever.

She gives the example that we should examine where the need ends and endless hoarding begins — earn for the children, then for their future, then for the grandchildren. Her intention is not to find fault with earning or planning; the main thing is to ask the question whether we have tied our entire peace to a result in the future.

Kamasura’s Sadhana, Obtaining the Boon, and the Bond with Trishna

In the story of Kamasura the speaker explains episodes such as upasana, acquiring power, and obtaining the boon. Even after the desire is fulfilled his aspiration does not stop. Mentioning his relationship with the character Trishna, she comments that when endless thirst is added to desire it becomes stronger.

Here Trishna appears not merely as a Puranic character but as a symbol of the inner pressure of “still more is needed.” If that pressure increases, the difference of “mine–yours,” comparisons, and the sense of domination may strengthen. She says that when the influence of desires on the mind and intellect increases, it becomes difficult to recognize what we really need.

Fulfilling Desire and Becoming a Slave to Desire

Wanting an object and striving to obtain it is one state. Feeling that life is wasted if it is not obtained is another state. Harika Suri clearly explains the difference between these two. To understand desire one must see how much space it occupies in our thoughts and what kind of decisions we are taking for its sake.

Mentioning the context of Vishvamitra, Vashishtha Maharshi, and Kamadhenu, she explains how the insistence on necessarily obtaining what others have increases on seeing it. One may try to fulfill a desire; but if that effort destroys our values, relationships, and peace, there is a need to examine it again, she suggests.

Family Life and Spiritual Practice — Place for Both

The speaker specially reminds that when interest in spiritual practice increases, family responsibilities should not be neglected. Even though activities such as meditation, study, and service are valuable, giving time to those who live with us is also part of life. She says not to turn spirituality into a way of escaping from the family.

Just as the time allotted for sadhana should be respected, the time for talking with family members and the time for listening to their needs should also be respected. In her teaching it appears that there is no need to completely leave one field for growth in another field; balance can be achieved with awareness.

Worship of Mayuresha and the Appearance of Vikata

In the story the suggestion comes that Mayuresha should be worshipped for the solution of the problems caused by Kamasura. Mentioning places such as Dandakaranya and Bhusvananda, Harika Suri explains the background related to Ganapati worship. Thereafter she introduces the episode in which Mayuresha appears in the form of Vikata.

She says that one should not stop only with the negative notions that usually come to the mind for the word “Vikata.” This form is explained as a symbol of the tattva that understands desires and removes their domination. The avatar in the story is closer to the question of how dissatisfaction and delusion within dissolve than to a display of power over outsiders.

The Peacock Vehicle — Acceptance, but Not Clinging

The speaker specially analyzes Vikata’s peacock vehicle. Comparing the peacock’s qualities such as beauty and attraction with the world of desires, she explains that completely rejecting them is not spirituality. Using it as a vehicle means driving them; not tying one’s identity with them — she states this symbolic meaning.

In our life there may be objects, relationships, and successes. They can be used and enjoyed. But one must come out of the state that if they are not there our inner peace completely collapses. This metaphor reminds us of the difference between acceptance and clinging.

Kamasura’s Power Decreasing Before Vikata

Harika Suri explains that in the story when the form of Vikata appears, Kamasura reaches a state in which he cannot use his power. For him who considered himself invincible, knowing his limitation is an important turn in the story. This episode indicates how temporary it is to think that with the notion that power exists we can control everything.

When the mind that runs behind desires stops for a moment and looks at itself, opportunity arises for new understanding. That is the beginning of self-inquiry. The speaker’s teaching suggests that change becomes possible by recognizing what kind of insistence desire is creating within us, without seeing desire as an enemy.

The Desire to Change Others Is Also Desire

In the final part of the discourse Harika Suri examines the strong desire that people we like should change some habits. Even if our intention is their welfare, if we insist that they must change exactly according to our words, that too can become a kind of desire for control, she suggests.

Giving information to others, helping, and showing a good path can be done. However, space should also be given to their journey and their decisions. She explains that observing how our expectations are working, rather than becoming angry that they did not change in the way we hoped, is part of spiritual maturity.

Awareness of Desires in Daily Life

This discourse invites us that when a desire arises in the mind, without immediately judging it as good or bad, we should examine “Is this a real need? Is it a habit? Did it come from comparison? Did it come from fear?” With this examination the decision whether to fulfill the desire, postpone it, or leave it can be taken more clearly.

What the Vikata avatar story finally reminds us of is not the command to live a life without desires. It is the question whether it is possible to live without becoming a slave to them while remaining amid desires, responsibilities, and relationships. Meditation, swadhyaya, acceptance, and balance with the family — the speaker shows all these as practical paths to that question.
