Before Changing Words We Should See Our Inner Self

Nirmala Kalepu introduced the topic of the third day in this series proceeding on the basis of the book Speak Peace in a World of Conflict. She explained that the manner of speaking with others is not merely the selection of polite words; change is needed in our thoughts and responses as well. When there is conflict it becomes difficult to speak peacefully; that is why the emotion that forms in us should be observed even before the moment the word comes out.

The speaker recalled the four stages of NVC discussed in previous classes: observation, feelings, needs, request. The first step is seeing what happened without judgment. The second is recognizing what feeling that event caused in us. The third is knowing the met or unmet need related to that feeling. Finally the fourth is making a clear request without blaming the other person or forcing them. The discourse suggests that this method is not for controlling others; it is useful for increasing understanding between us and them.

Marshall Rosenberg’s Aspiration: Conversation for Peace

The speaker did not see the work Marshall Rosenberg did merely as teaching a communication method. She explained it as the effort he made for connection to increase in place of violent words among people. It was mentioned in the discourse that the help that came for this service was used for the spread of NVC rather than for personal benefits. The speaker compared that dedication with the service attitude of Brahmarshi Patriji. This is a spiritual comparison made by the speaker; it is not a historical confirmation that the lives of both were the same.

The speaker reminded that even those who are doing meditation and spiritual practice have a need to question themselves whether there is compassion in their words. Do not think that merely because there is spiritual knowledge the response will always be peaceful. Only through applying what is learned in practice in the family, in school, and in daily conflicts does that knowledge come closer to life.

Learning from Now Without Blaming the Past

When occasions of having spoken with anger in the past come to memory, regret may arise in us. However, the speaker explained that holding on to that memory and punishing ourselves with “How bad am I?” can become another violent inner conversation. She said that what is important is to see how to move forward now with the knowledge that is known.

The speaker shared the viewpoint that people can naturally remain with love and mutual cooperation. She commented that under the influence of society, upbringing, and habits, criticisms, comparisons, and punishments become part of ordinary conversation. This thought should be read not as a historical dating, but as the discourse message of recognizing the compassion within us again. Even if we are unable to change the past, the present response can be changed.

Why a Different Response Each Time to the Same Mistake?

Sharing her experience in the education field, Nirmala did self-inquiry that even if a student makes a big mistake she is able to respond peacefully in some contexts, while in another context there is a possibility of responding intensely even to a small matter. She suggested that here the student’s behavior alone does not decide our anger. Our stress, expectations, and unmet needs at that moment also have an influence.

Therefore without stopping with the simple explanation “anger came to me because he did that,” one should ask oneself “What felt important to me at that time? Which need did I feel was not met?” This question does not give justification to the other person’s action; it opens the path of taking responsibility for our response.

The Mother Who Shouted at the Children: The Need Behind the Anger

As an example told in Rosenberg’s training, the speaker explained a context in which a mother spoke loudly with her daughter. That mother felt pain that she should have said it more gently. However, the discourse examined the manner in which questions were asked so as to know which need was not met for her at that moment, without ending that incident with the judgment “I am a bad mother.”

This example shows how anger increases when the feeling comes that a need was not met. At the same time this does not mean that the mother had no love for her daughter. Love, stress, and impatience can exist in us at the same time. If behavior is to be changed, not merely suppressing anger, but recognizing the need behind it and seeking the opportunity to speak more gently.

From Pain to Discrimination: The Teacher’s Words the Speaker Recalled

Nirmala recalled a sentence written by the physics teacher in the autograph book during her tenth-class days. She saw in those words the idea that pain makes a person think; thought increases discrimination; discrimination can make life more joyful. She connected this memory with the study of NVC.

Here the intention is not to praise pain as great. She explained that when pain arises, instead of abusing ourselves with “Why did I do like this?”, we can see “What is this incident teaching me?” Then space is formed to think how to respond if the same incident is faced again. Recognizing the mistake is necessary; stamping that the mistake itself is our personality is not necessary.

Mistakes Are Not Judgments; They Are Information for Learning

The speaker explained that when those who participated in Rosenberg’s training talk about the mistakes they made, they use self-blame words such as “I am a fool,” “I should not have done that.” She asked to examine whether such words are helping change or pulling us down further. She specially brought forward the idea of Learning without self-hatred.

Taking responsibility on seeing a mistake and hating ourselves are two different things. The first is useful for growth; the second may take our vision away from the solution. Learning with patience, saying apology if needed, and trying to respond newly in the next context — all these are parts of change with compassion. Nirmala also shared the matter that she tells students that when they do good work they should congratulate themselves. That encouragement increases the confidence that we are learning.

The Mother Who Is Getting Late: Respect, Time Management, Guilt

In another training example a mother shouted at the children with the worry that she was getting late for a program. Later she severely blamed herself that she was not a good mother. When speaking with her it became clear how important it was to her to reach on time and to respect those organizing the program. It came to light in the discussion that the fear that that need would not be fulfilled itself increased the stress at that time.

The main matter in this example is not justifying her shouting. After recognizing the value of attention toward time management, it is showing that a way of speaking more respectfully with the children can be found. The speaker explained that a person who loves the children can also hurt them under stress; while taking responsibility for that behavior one can come out from self-blame. She also connected her experience of wanting to reach QLU classes on time to this context.

From Self-Compassion to Understanding the Other Person’s Needs

The discourse says that after understanding the stress within us we can also listen to the other person’s needs. Stopping at the moment anger comes and recognizing our feeling, requesting instead of immediately ordering, and listening to the other person’s words — these are habits that grow with practice. Change may not become complete all at once; yet every context can be seen newly.

The speaker said that if peace is to be shared with others, peace should also be cultivated within our inner self. She explained this not merely as control of words, but as a practice of expressing the compassion within us outwardly. Continuing the awareness cultivated in meditation in daily conversations is close to this thought.

Listeners’ Questions: What If We Make a Mistake Even After Knowing?

In the final part of the discourse the listeners shared their experiences and doubts. Especially the question came of how we can remain without blaming ourselves when anger may come toward the children even though we are meditating and listening to Jnanodayam classes. This doubt brought out the real difficulty faced at the place of moving from theory to practice.

In the speaker’s answer matters such as awareness, recognition of needs, and trying again with compassion were said prominently. Without rejecting the matter that anger came, one should examine why it came. If a mistake has occurred an effort should be made to repair the relationship. At the same time the judgment “I will never change” should not be placed on us. It was revealed in the discussion that for lasting change to come in the other person, understanding that forms with compassion is important rather than being obedient on the surface out of fear.

Change Within Us Is the Beginning for Peaceful Conversation

The matter that was heard again and again in today’s teaching: compassion is needed not only for others, but also for us. Taking responsibility without blaming ourselves, recognizing the need behind the anger, and giving place to request in words — all these are small practices in the direction of improving mutual relationships. Even if we are unable to change others’ behavior immediately, we can observe our response and choose a new path.

In Nirmala Kalepu’s explanation the training examples in the book, school experiences, inspiration obtained from gurus, and listeners’ questions all take us toward one idea: change grows steadily with understanding, not with criticism. Listening to our words from today itself and knowing the feelings behind them can be the beginning for that practice.
