The Glory of Parashakti — The Spiritual Message Hidden in the Form of Mahishasuramardini

On the sixth day, Dr. Newton interprets the story of Mahishasuramardini as a map of inner transformation. Mahishasura's austerity, the image of the eighteen-armed Divine Mother, and the weapons she carries are considered through the lens of meditation and the tendencies that obstruct awareness. The teaching moves from the language of chakras and inner fears to the four states of consciousness, the four modes of closeness to the Divine, and the meaning of surrender. The trident becomes a concluding symbol of moving beyond the three gunas and recognizing a consciousness not confined to ordinary waking, dreaming, or deep sleep.

Main Teachings

Mahishasura — A Symbol Along the Meditative Path

Friends, the story of Mahishasura need not be approached only as a battle between a goddess and an asura. Dr. Newton invites listeners to see in it the challenges of the inner journey. The story of Mahishasura's austerity on Mount Meru is connected with the search for higher states of awareness.

People report different experiences in meditation. Some describe sensations, images, or a sense of activity associated with the third eye or Sahasrara; others do not. The teaching cautions against turning any one experience into a compulsory milestone. The lecture uses the traditional language of chakras, Sushumna, and the awakening of energy as part of its yogic interpretation, not as a claim that these are established anatomical mechanisms.

The Divine Energy and Our Inner Adversaries

The lecture relates survival-related fears to Muladhara, emotional patterns to Svadhishthana, and aggressive or dominating tendencies to Manipura within its spiritual framework. The important question is not whether we can name a chakra but whether we can recognize fear, attachment, anger, and the impulse to control.

A frightened animal may react defensively. Human beings, too, can respond to perceived threats with anger or aggression. Dr. Newton uses such observations to bring attention to patterns that meditation can help us notice. The battle with Mahishasura becomes a way of speaking about this inward work.

The Eighteen Arms — Many Powers, One Inner Transformation

The image of the Divine Mother with eighteen arms and many weapons can be read as a representation of the powers needed to overcome ignorance and inner bondage. In the story, the energies of the deities come together in the form of the Goddess. Dr. Newton relates this image to the awakening of awareness, strength, and clarity within the practitioner.

The weapons are not an instruction to fight other people. They point toward the transformation of fear, anger, delusion, and pride. The preserved source does not reliably establish a complete, individually verified meaning for every weapon in every hand; therefore no invented eighteen-item list is supplied here.

Waking, Dreaming, Deep Sleep, and Turiya

The lecture turns to jagrat (waking), svapna (dreaming), sushupti (deep sleep), and turiya, the fourth state described in contemplative traditions. During waking, the mind is occupied with the world of the senses. Dreaming brings another field of images and experiences. Deep sleep is distinguished from both. Turiya is discussed as awareness beyond identification with these changing conditions.

Meditative images, colors, or visionary experiences can feel meaningful to the person who has them. Yet the teaching directs attention beyond collecting experiences. The central question is whether the practitioner is becoming more aware, less bound by reactions, and more deeply established in stillness.

Four Ways of Approaching the Divine

Dr. Newton discusses salokya, samipya, sarupya, and sayujya as stages or modes of relationship with the Divine.

Salokya is explained through the sense of being in the same realm or world as the Divine. Samipya conveys nearness. Sarupya points to likeness or identification with the Divine form. Sayujya expresses union, the movement beyond the felt separation between devotee and the Divine Mother.

These terms are presented in the spiritual sense developed in the lecture. The emphasis is not on acquiring a new status or making a claim of superiority, but on allowing the feeling of separateness to soften.

Bringing the Teaching Into the Body and Attitude

The lecture links inward intention with posture and devotional gesture. The body can express reverence through bowing, and the mind can accompany that movement with sincere attention. A gesture made mechanically is different from one in which a person is consciously offering pride and attachment.

In this sense, spiritual practice is not merely a set of movements. Its meaning depends on awareness, humility, and the quality of surrender behind the action.

Devi Samarpana — Surrender Without Separation

The practice of Devi Samarpana is presented as an offering of the limited sense of 'I' to the Divine Mother. Rather than holding tightly to the identity of the one who performs, controls, or achieves, the practitioner is invited to bow with openness.

The deeper movement is from seeing the Mother as entirely outside oneself toward recognizing an inseparable relationship with that presence. This is the devotional and nondual direction in which the lecture develops its teaching.

The Trident — Going Beyond the Three Gunas

The trident, Trishula, is interpreted as a symbol of the three gunas: tamas, rajas, and sattva. The aim of practice is to recognize their influence without remaining bound by them. Dr. Newton relates this to trigunatita, the state beyond identification with the three gunas.

The three points are also associated in the lecture's symbolic explanation with waking, dreaming, and deep sleep. The teaching then turns toward Turiya, the awareness not limited to any one of those states. The trident is therefore not merely a weapon in an external story; it serves as a reminder of the inner journey beyond habitual conditioning.

The sixth day's teaching closes on the movement from separation to surrender and from surrender toward union: not simply to admire the Divine Mother as an image, but to allow the qualities she represents to transform one's life.
