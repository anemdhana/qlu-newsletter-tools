The Deva–Asura Natures Within Us — The Life-Truth Hidden Behind Harmony and Discord

Today, let us explore who the suras and asuras are. They are not merely characters in Puranic stories. They also represent harmony and disharmony, divine qualities and unhelpful tendencies within us. From the rhythm of breath and the three gunas to Mahadurga, Mahalakshmi, and Mahasaraswati, and from the power of intention to the deeper desire beneath our wishes, there is much to discover.

Speaker: Brahmarshi Chakravarthy Dr. Newton Kondaveti
Topic: Devi Navratri Wisdom — From the Deva–Asura Principle to Intention and Surrender
Original Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CgTrk319L1Q

01. Suras and Asuras — When Rhythm Becomes Discord

We speak of swara and apaswara in speech and music. If we say something inappropriate to the occasion, it creates discord. If a musical note falls out of rhythm, we call it apaswara. Similarly, life has its own rhythm, order, and connection.

When our body, mind, heart, and inner being are in harmony, we live with greater balance. When that connection is lost, discord arises within. Let us understand the Puranic imagery of suras and asuras through this comparison.

This is a spiritual interpretation, not a claim about the historical etymology of the words sura and asura.

02. Breath as Swara — Observing the Rhythm of Life

Breath is the closest spiritual instrument we have. It supports speech and the body's essential activities. Yogic teachings give it particular significance when speaking about prana shakti.

Observing breath brings us towards the present moment. It helps us notice the relationship between our body, mind, and feelings. By attending to its natural rhythm, we can become more aware of our inner state.

Before trying to change the breath, observe it. Meditation helps us recognise the discord within and cultivate greater harmony.

03. Birth Does Not Determine Spiritual Greatness

Consider Ravana. He practised austerities, mastered music, and acquired knowledge. Yet arrogance, possessiveness, and harmful tendencies remained within him. Spiritual practice or learning alone does not guarantee divine conduct.

We cannot determine a person's spiritual stature by birth. The real measure is Self-knowledge and the recognition of the same Self in all beings. When we experience the truth expressed as Sarvam Khalvidam Brahma, our actions naturally turn towards the wellbeing of the world.

Continuous practice, service, and wishing for the happiness of all beings are qualities worth cultivating.

04. The Three Gunas — Tamas, Rajas, and Sattva

When tamas predominates, we may become lethargic, avoid necessary work, or act from anger, violence, or pride. Even our good actions may become entangled in a constant desire for praise.

When rajas becomes excessive, we may chase recognition, fame, and prestige without rest. Working and achieving are not wrong. The problem begins when the craving for recognition takes away our peace. Anger may subside for a while, only to return in the same pattern.

Cultivating sattva means developing clarity, balance, service, and inward awareness. The first step is to observe the qualities operating within us.

Central insight: Before looking for asuras outside, observe how ego, possessiveness, aggression, anger, and the desire for praise operate within yourself.

The King Who Had to Remove His Inner Crown

Consider the story of a king who visited a teacher seeking Self-knowledge. He arrived wearing a heavy golden crown, armour, and jewels. When the teacher told him to remove the burdens he carried, the king removed these ornaments. But the teacher meant something deeper: remove the crown in your mind—the conviction that “I am the king; I am greater than others.” Taking off outward symbols does not remove inner pride.

Similarly, a person may resolve to avoid anger for a few days or even forty days. If the inner pattern remains unchanged, anger can return. Spiritual change requires releasing attachment within, not only observing an external rule for a short time.

05. The Deva–Asura Battle Is Within Us

The Puranas often describe battles between devas and asuras. Do not read these stories only as external events. They also point to a struggle between tendencies within ourselves.

Even while meditating, we may experience jealousy, anger, attachment, and pride. One day we are peaceful; the next day, a small incident makes us angry. This is the inner struggle.

Meditation is a movement towards our divine nature. Which qualities are growing in us? Which are carrying us away from our deeper Self? Keep asking these questions.

06. Ravana's Story — Practice Does Not Excuse Crossing Boundaries

Ravana performed great austerities. Yet he violated Sita Mata's freedom by taking her away without her consent. Failing to respect another person's autonomy and consent is an example of asuric conduct.

No matter how much knowledge, power, or skill we acquire, how we use it matters. Spiritual practice should make us more humble and responsible.

07. The Three Stages of Navratri

During Navratri, we remember three principal forms of Shakti.

The first three days — Mahadurga: Awaken the will to overcome obstacles, limiting patterns, and difficulties that repeatedly hold us back.

The next three days — Mahalakshmi: Cultivate divine qualities, confidence, inner resources, and a sense of abundance.

The final three days — Mahasaraswati: Move towards discernment, clarity, and Self-knowledge.

These stages belong to one spiritual journey: recognise and overcome obstacles; nurture noble qualities; become established in wisdom.

08. Why Do We Turn to the Mother?

As children, when we needed something, we often went first to our mother. When we were hurt or troubled, we told her. I remember wanting a toy as a child. It felt difficult to ask my father directly, so I would approach my mother. Somehow, she would speak to him and help.

Let us bring that feeling of closeness to Jaganmata. Offer our difficulties, obstacles, and limitations to that Shakti. We approach Mahadurga as the power that helps us cross what seems difficult.

Here, the Mother is not merely an external deity. We are also remembering the Adi Shakti within ourselves.

To Be Close to the Self Is to Live in Harmony

We can describe the divine tendency in another way: the closer we come to our own Self, the more we live in harmony. When we identify ourselves only with the physical body and pursue every outward attraction, we move away from that inner connection. Beauty, wealth, and possessions change. Building our identity entirely around them gives rise to pride, attachment, attraction, and aversion. Meditation helps us return to the Self.

Fasting from Mental Noise and Social Media

During these nine days, fasting need not refer only to food. The discourse emphasises mind fasting—reducing unnecessary thought and cultivating inner silence—and social-media fasting—taking a break from reels, constant phone use, and distracting entertainment. Let us reclaim time and energy for practice. The lecture also mentions reducing meals, but dietary restriction should be adapted to individual health needs. Children, pregnant people, and anyone with diabetes or other medical conditions should not undertake restrictive fasting without appropriate medical advice.

09. Ichha Shakti — Give Energy a Direction

Desire itself is not wrong. Without intention, how would we act? But we need to discover what we truly seek beneath our desires.

Which obstacles keep returning? What limitations prevent us from progressing in practice? Recognise them clearly.

Energy needs direction. Write down your goals. Even if your ultimate life purpose is not yet clear, identify the direction in which you want to grow now. Do not let your energy scatter without intention.

Work for the welfare of the world also needs intention and action, whether through teaching meditation, promoting vegetarianism, or serving through a project.

10. What Do I Really Want?

Ask yourself, “What do I want?” The first answer may not be the deepest one. Behind one desire lies another; behind that lies a feeling we wish to experience.

For example: “I want this family conflict resolved.” Why? “I want peace of mind.” Why? “Then I can meditate more.” Why meditate more? “So I can remain connected with myself.” What does that bring? Inner joy and fulfilment.

Keep asking until the feeling beneath the outward wish becomes clear.

Reflection: Ask, “If this happens, what will I gain?” Repeating this question can reveal the deeper experience you are truly seeking.

11. A Participant's Example — From Family Stability to Inner Fulfilment

A participant shared her wish that her husband obtain a good job and a higher position. He had the qualifications, she explained, and she felt that if the family became more secure, she could progress spiritually.

Let us look more closely. What would a better job for her husband actually give her? Stability, fewer worries, time for practice, and ultimately a feeling of fulfilment.

The outward wish may be for employment; the deeper longing may be for peace, contentment, and the joy of the Self. Recognising this can help us approach our intentions with greater awareness.

12. Gurus, Intentions, and Spiritual Desires

Some desires concern worldly life. Others concern spiritual growth: enlightenment, liberation, opening the third eye, or experiencing higher spiritual states.

A traditional spiritual story involving Lahiri Mahasaya and Mahavatar Babaji was mentioned to illustrate the relationship between a guru, a disciple's desire, and moving beyond desire. This is presented as a traditional spiritual narrative, not as an independently verified historical event.

Whatever the scale of our intention, remember to ask: “What is it that I truly want?”

13. Surrender to Mahadurga — Nine Bowing Cycles

Let us revisit the Devi Samarpana practice introduced on Day 2. The demonstration again includes sitting posture, breathing through a selected nostril, namaskara mudra, bowing the head, and inwardly remembering the seed syllable Shreem.

The discourse recommends nine cycles. In each cycle, the most important element is not the bodily movement but the feeling of offering the ego to Jaganmata. Imagine placing your desires, obstacles, and intentions at the feet of Mahadurga.

After the practice, remain still and silent, allowing the feeling of surrender to settle.

Safety note: The source demonstration includes closing a nostril, staged inhalation, breath retention, and bending the head. These techniques may not be safe for everyone. People with neck or spine conditions, heart or respiratory problems, or dizziness should not attempt them without appropriate in-person professional guidance. You can cultivate the feeling of surrender while sitting comfortably and breathing naturally.

14. Surrender Must Be Accompanied by Action

Offering our intentions to the Divine Mother does not mean abandoning our responsibilities. We must still do what is ours to do. Take the practical steps that correspond to your intention, even if those steps are small.

Move forward with the trust that the rest is offered to Shakti. This attitude can help us act without being overwhelmed by fear.

Surrender is not passivity. Practice, intention, action, and acceptance must work together.

15. Questions — Family Property, Unity, and Service

A participant described a family property matter. She wished for unity among five sisters, a resolution of the matter, and the opportunity to use the outcome for the welfare of others. She also expressed intentions for financial abundance, peace, and liberation.

Let us remember that it is not wrong to seek solutions to external matters. But peace, unity, and service must grow within us too. Spiritual intention should accompany responsible family communication and necessary practical steps.

16. Childhood Illness — “Training, Not Punishment”

Nagaveni raised an important question. We speak of karma as training rather than punishment. But what about young children with serious or chronic illnesses, who do not yet have the understanding to interpret their suffering spiritually? How can it be called training?

In response, the discourse emphasised that children must not be forced to meditate. When parents practise themselves, children may naturally imitate them. Give children freedom and lead by example.

The speaker also presented a spiritual belief that souls plan lessons before birth. This is a spiritual worldview expressed in the lecture, not a medical explanation of childhood illness.

Important clarification: A child's illness must not be treated as proof of wrongdoing, deserved punishment, or a burden the child chose. Adults remain responsible for providing appropriate medical care, love, protection, and support.

The Role of Adults in Caring for Children

In answering Nagaveni's question, the discourse explains that children learn under the care of parents and teachers. When a child experiences illness, adults must provide the care that the child needs. The speaker uses the example of an autistic child to emphasise unconditional love, patience, and understanding. Children may voluntarily imitate adults who meditate, but they should not be forced to meditate.

The discussion also includes spiritual interpretations involving karma, reincarnation, past-life relationships, and situations supposedly co-created by family members. These are matters of spiritual belief, not established causes of childhood illness, and must not be used to blame children or parents. Medical care, developmental support, and family support remain essential.

17. The Power of Gurus and Respect for Individual Freedom

The later discussion referred to revered teachers, including Neem Karoli Baba, and accounts of extraordinary events associated with them. It also emphasised respecting each soul's journey and freedom.

Such accounts belong to spiritual tradition. They do not replace medical treatment or scientific assessment.

18. A Devotional Song to the Mother

Following the questions, a devotional song to the Divine Mother was performed. The captions include phrases such as Omkararupini, Kleemkara Vasini, and Prakriti Swarupini. The gathering concluded by remembering the presence of Jaganmata through music.

The Central Teaching of Day 3

Suras and asuras are within us. Observe the rhythm of breath. Recognise pride, attachment, anger, and the craving for recognition; move towards service, balance, and Self-knowledge. Invoke Mahadurga to face obstacles, Mahalakshmi to nurture divine qualities, and Mahasaraswati to cultivate wisdom. Discover the deeper fulfilment behind your desires, and combine surrender with responsible action.
