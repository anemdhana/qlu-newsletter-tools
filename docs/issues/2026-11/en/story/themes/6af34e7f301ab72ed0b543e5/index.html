Lambodara — The Tattvartha Behind the Large Belly

Harika Suri says that when we hear the name Lambodara we usually remember Ganapati’s large belly. However, she explains that if we take the forms in Puranic stories merely as physical characteristics, their inner meaning may be missed. In this avatar story the belly stands as a symbol related to a vast cosmic vision.

When our attention revolves around small events, small victories, and small insults, we forget how vast the universe is. The speaker brings forward the form of Lambodara as an indication that reminds us of that vastness. She says that if that vision arises, our anger, impulse, and the insistence of “I alone” are themselves questioned.

Birth of Krodhasura — When Anger Becomes a Tendency

Harika Suri explains how Krodhasura was born in the story related to the Lambodara avatar. She comments that this character indicates anger born in an event becoming an independently strengthening tendency. Even though anger first appears as a reaction, if it is repeatedly nourished it becomes a power that influences our thoughts and decisions.

She suggests that the story of Krodhasura should be compared with the impulse within us. When anger comes, our own words appear correct to us; the space to understand the other person’s situation decreases. She explains that if there is no awareness at this time, a momentary emotion can damage long-term relationships.

Is Sankalpa for Selfishness or for Collective Good?

Explaining how important the intention is in making a sankalpa, the speaker gives examples from the lives of great beings. Referring to gurus such as Dr. Newton, Dr. Lakshmi, Patriji, Buddha, and Jesus, she says that a great sankalpa desires the welfare of others beyond personal benefit.

She reminds us that when power, opportunity, and knowledge come to us we can shape them so that they are useful to others. If anger and selfishness shrink our vision, the sankalpa made for collective good expands our vision. Observing this difference is an important part of spiritual practice, in her view.

Why Does the Past Good Not Appear in Anger?

Harika Suri explains that when anger is intense, the help done by the other person in the past, the love shown, and the experiences shared may all not come to memory at once. At that moment the mind makes one event large and sees it. Therefore she suggests that anger may not show us the complete truth.

This matter appears clearly in family and in friendships. If one word is disliked it may feel as though the entire relationship should be rejected. But after the impulse decreases we are able to see the same event more balanced. That is why her teaching suggests that opportunity should be given to awareness before taking decisions at a time when emotion is heightened.

Symbols of Krodha, Priti, and Family Relationships

In the story of Krodhasura the speaker also examines the names of other characters. Especially she mentions how feelings that appear opposite, such as krodha and priti, exist together in the same life. We may love someone and yet become angry with them; impulse may appear more in close relationships.

Therefore merely because anger has come we cannot conclude that there is no love; likewise we cannot justify the harm done with anger in the name of love being present. What is needed is to observe honestly the feelings that are working in us at the same time. She explains that by seeing the Puranic characters as symbols of these mental conflicts the story comes closer to present-day life.

The Peak of Anger, the Fall of Impulse

Harika Suri explains in the context of the story that when any tendency reaches its peak its change begins. When anger increases we may speak harsh words, hurt those close to us, and later feel regret. Only after the impulse decreases does the effect of the actions we have done become clearly visible.

From her teaching it is understood that merely suppressing anger is not the solution; observing it while it is in the stage of increasing is important. If we are able to ask “In what state is my mind now?” we can see that impulse does not completely gain mastery over our actions.

Anger Melts Before Lambodara’s Vishvarupa

In the story the vision of Lambodara’s vishvarupa comes as a crucial episode. The speaker explains that when Krodhasura, who considered himself very great, comes to know how small the creation he knew is, his ahankara and impulse decrease. Referring to the vastness of many brahmandas, she conveys the message that a person should not regard his small range as the whole truth.

When we regard our problem as the whole world it appears unbearable. When seen with a vast vision the same problem finds a different place. She explains that this universal vision is not rejecting pain; it is a spiritual perspective that helps change our response to be more balanced.

Satya Darshana and Coming Out of Dualities

The speaker says that when the mind is caught in the duality of “I am right, the other is wrong,” conflict continues. When truth is grasped more deeply it is known that the opinions we hold are not complete. Krodhasura’s transformation is connected with this understanding.

Satya darshana does not mean merely seeing a wonderful form; it also means recognizing how ahankara, limited vision, and impulse are working within us. Then the readiness to change our response increases more than the insistence on changing others. This stands as the practical lesson in the story.

The Fruit of Meditation — Awareness in Every Action

Harika Suri makes it clear that the fact that we are meditating is not known merely from the minutes we sit for meditation. How much awareness is present in us while walking, talking, working, and being with family members is the important measure. Meditation should appear in our lifestyle.

Being able to observe anger when it comes, being able to stop speech when needed, accepting if a mistake has occurred, and desiring the welfare of others — all these can be seen as results of practice, she says. What the Lambodara avatar story reminds us of is not only how vast the universe is, but also the question of to what extent that vastness is reflected in our daily behavior.
