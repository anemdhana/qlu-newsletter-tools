Why Gratitude Is a Powerful Practice

In the final meeting of the Speak Peace series the speaker described gratitude as a strong transformational practice. If attention is placed only on what we do not have, dissatisfaction increases; if we recognize the experiences, persons, and opportunities that nourished our life, we can see relationships from another angle. The speaker explained this change in the language of spiritual power.

Clear Gratitude Instead of Praise

The speaker explained that stating the specific action someone did, how our need was met because of it, and what feeling arose in us is deeper gratitude than merely praising someone as a good person. The understanding of observation, feeling, and need in NVC is useful here as well. Gratitude stated in this way clearly makes known to the other person the effect of their action.

Humility Does Not Mean Reducing One’s Own Value

The speaker specially spoke about humbleness or humility. This discussion suggests that rejecting the power and capacity within oneself is not true humility; while recognizing others’ cooperation one should also be able to accept one’s own value. Recognizing the help that came to us is gratitude; respecting the capacity we have is self-respect. The two are not mutually opposed.

Cooperation Visible in Small Experiences

There are contexts shared by the speaker and participants about small events such as providing food and helping in daily works. Behind the services that ordinarily pass without being noticed there is also someone’s time, attention, and effort. When they are recognized the understanding increases that we are not living alone. The message is expressed here that gratitude should not be limited only to big events.

The Language with Which One Speaks to Oneself

Discussion occurred about the habit of severely blaming ourselves when a mistake has occurred. Even though taking responsibility for the action that happened is necessary, continuous self-blame may not help change. Recognizing the mistake, saying apology if needed, and learning how to respond with more attention thereafter is a path closer to self-empathy.

Gratitude Practice at the End of the Day

The speaker mentioned recognizing the help we received, the assistance we provided to others, and the lessons learned by recalling the events that happened in the day. In this practice priority can be given to small true experiences rather than great victories. With the message that NVC, beginning as a conversation method, should finally become part of our vision, relationships, and way of life, this series ends.

The Difference Between Praise and Gratitude

Deepti Reddy explained that praising someone with “you are a very great person” and stating gratitude with “because of this work you did this help came to me” are not the same. The first word gives a value judgment to the person; the second makes known the action that happened, the need met because of it, and the feeling that arose in us. She said that in NVC gratitude should be shared as a clear human experience.

The speaker also mentioned the context of teaching behavior to children on the basis of rewards and punishments. She commented that there is a danger that children may feel that their value depends only on others’ approval or on achievements. She suggested that without using gratitude as a reward, stating the joy that really arose because of cooperation is closer to the relationship.

Changed Conversation in a Mother’s Life

The speaker told the example of a mother who was pained that the relationship with her son had become distant and who, after learning NVC, obtained the hope that connection with him may happen again. When that mother shared her experience, gratitude was expressed through saying which teaching was useful to her and what change appeared in her life because of it.

Here gratitude did not remain merely as the polite word “thank you.” It became a conversation that makes known which need one person’s words fulfilled in another person’s life. The speaker explained that clearly stating that effect to the person who helped us makes the experience meaningful for both.

The Language with Which We Speak to Ourselves

The speaker specially mentioned the habit of abusing ourselves when we make a mistake. When we speak to ourselves with “Do I have no sense?” the pain, regret, and the need to do better that are behind that word can be recognized. Responsibility does not increase by insulting oneself; there is opportunity to learn by accepting the action that happened and deciding the subsequent change.

She said that if we are able to speak with compassion to ourselves the same language may also come naturally toward others. A person who places harsh judgment on himself finds it easy to place such judgments on others as well. Therefore observing self-conversation is not only a small personal practice in NVC, but a foundation for change in relationships.

Turning Gratitude into Daily Practice

On the final day of this series the speaker invited that gratitude should be turned into daily practice rather than as a feeling remembered occasionally. She said that the help that came to us, the cooperation we provided to others, and the small opportunities visible in ordinary life can be recognized. She also spoke about giving priority to gratitude in the list of goals.

What is important in this practice is not the number of words, but their honesty. If we recall what work happened, which need was met for us because of it, and how it felt to us, gratitude can become a habit. Bringing the awareness that grows in meditation practice to daily relationships is the spiritual connection in this concluding teaching.
