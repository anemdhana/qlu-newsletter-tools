Is It Possible to Speak with Peace Amid Differences?

While introducing a new nine-day series in the Jnanodayam program, it was recalled at the beginning of the program how the Nonviolent Communication classes held earlier had an impact on the lives of the participants. Differences naturally appear in the family, in society, and throughout the world. Talking to each other again after a quarrel is one matter; truly letting go of the remaining pain, judgments, and misunderstandings in that quarrel is another matter. It was explained that Marshall Rosenberg’s work Speak Peace in a World of Conflict was taken as the basis to examine the difference between these two.

Kiran’s discourse invited us to see this matter not only as theory but as practice. To speak with peace one must first recognize what is happening within us. From the points he explained it is known that the effort to understand our response, rather than the effort to change the other person, can open a new possibility in relationships.

Two Questions That Guided Marshall Rosenberg

Kiran introduced Marshall Rosenberg’s life and the inspirations that led to his research. What is the reason a person wants to hurt another person? What is it that inspires the same person to desire another’s welfare and to help him? It was said that these two questions occupied the main place in Rosenberg’s thoughts.

It was explained that with the interest of knowing the answer to these questions he studied psychology. Thereafter he developed a conversational method useful for resolving conflicts between individuals. The manner in which practices first held with small groups later expanded to more workshops was also mentioned in the discourse. The central idea of this method related to mutual understanding is that it should be useful not merely for winning in argument, but for listening to one person’s pain and another person’s need.

The Example of the Grandmother Who Lived Compassion

Kiran specially mentioned the compassion shown by Rosenberg’s grandmother in his childhood. Once a person in torn clothes and hungry came to their house. Seeing his condition the grandmother invited him inside and helped him to clean himself and obtain food; this incident was explained. This incident was taken not merely as a story related to charity, but as an example of the vision that can see the other person with their human needs rather than with a label.

Later in the questions and answers a participant expressed doubt about the trust needed to take such an unknown person into the house. That doubt reminds us of the delicate relationship that exists among compassion, trust, and caution. The compassionate viewpoint stated in the discourse should be read not as a rule that everyone must necessarily follow the same action, but as an invitation to understand people’s situation without first diminishing them.

Observation Is Different, Judgment Is Different

The first key practice in Nonviolent Communication is being able to state what happened without mixing in judgment. To show this difference Kiran explained the example of one person describing another as ‘lazy.’ ‘She is lazy’ is not the event that happened; it is the label we placed on that event. Details of what the person did, what they did not do, and in what context it happened are closer to observation.

Here the goal is not merely changing words. When we truly believe the label we have placed on someone, it becomes difficult to see their other qualities. When the same thing that happened is stated specifically, space is created for conversation. The remark ‘you are always like this’ may increase argument; mentioning a specific context may help mutual understanding.

Roommate Light Example: What Is Behind the Words?

An important practice that came in the discourse is related to a roommate who keeps the light on in the room at night. First a person expressed dissatisfaction with his roommate. When asked to state it more clearly, the event that ‘at bedtime the roommate keeps the light on without turning it off’ came out. This is not a judgment on the person’s nature; it is a description related to observable behavior.

The next question is more important: How are you feeling at that time? What feelings are arising in you because the light is on? This question takes the conversation from the level of ‘he is doing wrong’ to the level of ‘what is happening in me?’ It is understood that needs such as sleep, rest, or mutual consideration may be related here. However, only the person himself should recognize which need is primary for him; outsiders should not decide by guessing.

Are We Stating a Feeling or Making an Accusation?

Kiran explained that while thinking we are expressing the feelings that have arisen in us, many times we state a judgment on the other person in another form. For example, in the sentence ‘I felt as though I was insulted’ there may also be a meaning placed on the other person’s action along with our inner experience. Therefore the real feeling behind those words should be recognized more carefully.

Recognizing feelings such as anger, pain, disappointment, worry, happiness, and gratitude is a practice. The discourse suggests that rather than dividing feelings into positive and negative and suppressing some, we should see what information they are giving us. Even if the right word is not found immediately, asking ourselves ‘Is this really my feeling, or is it my decision about someone else?’ is the first step.

Met and Unmet Needs Behind Feelings

Why does joy arise in one context and pain in another? How are feelings related to our needs? Kiran expanded these questions. It was explained that along with physical needs such as food, water, rest, and touch, psychological needs such as respect, connection, and understanding are also important in our life. It was mentioned that in spiritual life too there are needs related to our inner experiences.

A small infant experiences its need even though it cannot state it in words. Even after becoming adults the needs do not disappear; but we often lose the capacity to recognize them. There is a possibility that the dissatisfaction within us increases further because of feeling ‘my need has no value.’ The discourse emphasized that recognizing needs is not equal to selfishness; needs are natural for a living person.

While Living for Others Are We Forgetting Our Needs?

Kiran examined some messages learned from childhood from family and society. Even though fulfilling the other person’s needs is good, if the habit forms of regarding stating one’s own needs as selfishness, the connection with our inner self may decrease. In this context it can be recognized that accepting one’s need and forcing another person to fulfill it are not the same.

By recognizing needs clarity arises as to why our feelings are coming. Understanding ‘I need respect’ is different from judging ‘you are bad because you did not respect me.’ This difference may reduce the conflict within us and show a new path for speaking with the other person.

Incident on the Road: One Behavior Is Not the Whole Person

In the questions and answers a participant mentioned the anger that arises on seeing the behavior of other vehicle drivers while traveling on a scooter. The doubt was expressed of how to understand the response changing in such contexts even though there is meditation and spiritual understanding. In response Kiran explained how useful it is to recognize our need.

On seeing a person driving a vehicle dangerously on the road one may generalize ‘everyone in this society is like this.’ But what we saw is only one aspect of that person’s behavior. He may have other good qualities in other contexts. When it is recognized that impatience arose because the need for our safety, orderliness, or mutual consideration was not met, it becomes easier to take appropriate action for the situation without condemning the person wholly, he explained.

Questions and Answers: Compassion, Meditation Awareness, Permission for Feelings

The members’ questions connected the main teaching to various contexts in life. The doubt that arose on the story related to the grandmother’s compassion created an opportunity to examine our vision toward strangers. In another question a participant explained about feeling as though awareness decreased in the middle while going into experiences during meditation. This led to thought on the difference between observing inner experiences and giving them meaning.

Someone else shared the experience of tears suddenly coming on seeing the form of Vinayaka. In that context Kiran importantly said that without immediately going to a decision of why the feeling came, one should recognize that the sorrow or emotion has arisen and give permission to it. Being in connection with the aliveness within us does not mean accepting only joy; it also means being able to observe pain.

The First Practice for Peaceful Conversation

Today’s discourse as a whole indicates a sequence: first observe what happened without judgment; then recognize the feeling that arose in us; then come to know the need behind that feeling. With the same vision, when we listen to the other person we are able to see that there may also be some need behind their behavior. With that one can move from a conversation that proves wrong to a conversation that leads to mutual understanding.

This is not a skill that is completed with one class. Repeated practice is needed in the way we speak with family members, with colleagues, with persons we encounter while traveling, and also with ourselves. As Kiran explained, peace is not only a situation without differences; it is the effort to maintain human connection while recognizing our feelings and needs with awareness even amid differences.
