One Parabrahma Principle — Many Divine Names

The teaching began with the idea that Indian festivals and forms of deities should not be seen merely as different traditions of worship, but as opportunities to understand the same truth from various angles. Referring to the Vedic statement ‘Ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti,’ it was explained that the all-pervading consciousness can also be recognized in the form of Vinayaka. It was suggested that before celebrating a festival it is important to know its spiritual significance.

Vinayaka, Gajamukha, and the Mouse Vehicle

The name ‘Vinayaka’ was explained as the supreme principle beyond whom there is no leader, and also as the power that develops self-discipline (atma shikshana) within us. The elephant-faced form was applied as a symbol of the state that has gone beyond ego, and the mouse as a symbol of the nature of possessiveness — hiding, hoarding, and holding on to ‘mine.’ Making the mouse one’s vehicle means not becoming a slave to possessiveness but gaining mastery over it.

Vakratunda, Ganapati, Vighneshwara

The curved trunk was explained as the form of Pranava, connected with breath practice, and also as a symbol of the power that corrects crooked intellect, crooked path, and the obstacles in life. In the name ‘Ganapati’ the inner meaning of knowledge and bliss was shown. It was said that the obstacles encountered in life sometimes become the cause for self-inquiry and for entering the path of meditation, and Vighneshwara tattva was analyzed extensively.

Siddhi–Buddhi, Labha–Kshema, and Ganadhipatya

The names Siddhi and Buddhi were connected with the spiritual capacity, discrimination (viveka), and Buddha-like state that blossom in the seeker. The names Labha and Kshema were explained with the meanings of obtaining what is to be obtained and protecting what has been obtained. Saying that ‘gana’ means group, it was explained that Ganapati is the symbol of the consciousness that functions in all groups, from the groups of cells in the body to living beings.

Inner Meaning of Chandra Darshana and the Syamantaka Mani Stories

On the occasion of Vinayaka Chavithi the custom of not looking at the moon, the accusations of Nilapani, and the Syamantaka Mani stories related to Sri Krishna were mentioned. It was suggested that these should not be taken merely as external events, but that the spiritual symbols in them should be examined. Aspects such as the qualification required to wear the Syamantaka Mani and conquering principles in the battle with Jambavan were applied to the seeker’s inner journey.

Atma Pradakshina and Muladhara Tattva

Ganesha performing pradakshina around Shiva and Parvati was explained as Atma pradakshina — seeing the divine consciousness within us. There was discussion about Ganapati tattva in the muladhara, the awakening of Kundalini Shakti, and the qualification required for sadhana. The message was given that traveling inward, purifying the mind through meditation, and grasping the inner meaning of the Puranas is the true festival practice.

Vinayaka Chavithi — A Festival of Knowledge in Practice

At the conclusion of the teaching it was suggested that Vinayaka Chavithi should be celebrated meaningfully through meditation, listening to knowledge, and self-inquiry, not limited only to outer festivities. The idea was mainly established that knowing Ganesha tattva means traveling in the direction of awakening the knowledge, bliss, humility, and self-control within us.
