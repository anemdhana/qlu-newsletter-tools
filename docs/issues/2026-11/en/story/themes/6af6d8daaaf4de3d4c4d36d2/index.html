Wisdom Alone Is the Weapon of Victory — The Inner Teaching in the Story of Shumbha and Nishumbha

Meditation, awareness, trust, surrender, and discernment are the means Dr. Newton emphasizes. The true test of spiritual learning is whether what we understand begins to change how we live.

The Final Three Days: Awakening the Power of Wisdom

Dr. Newton Kondaveti connects the final three days of Navratri with the awakening of jnana shakti, the power of wisdom. He describes spiritual practice through three complementary powers: iccha shakti (will), kriya shakti (action), and jnana shakti (wisdom), recalling the forms of Mahadurga, Mahalakshmi, and Mahasaraswati. The inner purification cultivated over the first six days prepares the seeker to recognize deeper obstacles with the sword of discernment.

He encourages listeners not to read the battles in the Devi Mahatmyam merely as episodes from mythology. The asuras also represent tendencies operating within the human mind. A story may capture our imagination, but its teaching becomes transformative only when we recognize its relevance to our own lives and put that understanding into practice.

Shumbha and Nishumbha: The Bondage of Pleasure and Pain

The speaker interprets Shumbha as a symbol of attachment to pleasure and Nishumbha as a symbol of attachment to suffering. The urge to secure pleasant experiences and the struggle to avoid painful ones can keep the mind turning in the same circle. He relates vritti, a mental tendency, to the image of a recurring circle, drawing attention to the repetitive character of such patterns.

When pleasure arrives, we want it to remain. Fear of its disappearance can itself become suffering. In suffering, we begin searching for pleasure again. The seeker is therefore invited to examine the ego and possessiveness that sustain this cycle, rather than assuming that the answer lies in obtaining ever more favorable experiences.

Chanda, Munda, Dhumralochana, and Raktabija

The other asuras in Shumbha's retinue are likewise presented as inner tendencies. Chanda points toward self-deception. Munda represents the self-defeating impulse that can undo hard-won progress at the last moment. Dr. Newton illustrates this with the image of someone climbing ninety-nine steps only to slip at the final one.

The name Dhumralochana evokes smoke. Just as smoke obscures our sight, the veil of illusion can cause the mind to mistake what is present for what is absent, and what is absent for what is present. Raktabija represents an even subtler multiplication of ego. In the myth, every drop of his blood gives rise to another Raktabija; inwardly, one small egoic impulse may proliferate into many forms.

These adversaries need not be sought somewhere outside us. Their traces can be observed in our thoughts, reactions, and habits. Recognizing them is the first movement toward freedom from their influence.

There Is No Surrender Without Trust

To support this inner transformation, the speaker emphasizes gathering and directing one's energy through meditation. Trust in the power within, a felt connection to that power, and faith in Devi Shakti form the ground of surrender. Without genuine trust, surrender may remain only a spoken ideal.

It is not enough simply to say that one meditates. The more revealing question is whether meditation has changed one's anger, pride, and self-defeating habits. If spirituality becomes a label or a mask, its deeper purpose is missed. Even family members, Dr. Newton observes, are more likely to trust visible changes in conduct than claims about spiritual attainment.

The Sculptor: Ego Hidden Behind Skill

Dr. Newton tells of a gifted sculptor who fashioned beautiful golden figures and grew proud of his skill, believing that no one could equal him. When a sage visited, the sculptor proudly displayed his work. The sage smiled and pointed to a small clay oil lamp in the corner. However beautiful the golden figures might be, they could break if dropped and could be stolen. The humble clay lamp, by contrast, held a flame that dispelled darkness, without pride or fear. The sage compared the sculptor’s fragile ego to the golden figures and urged him to kindle the lamp of Self-knowledge.

The speaker uses the story to expose subtle forms of ego. Pride may arise from wealth, position, physical beauty, academic degrees, intellectual ability, professional excellence, or even spiritual practice. The lesson is not to reject accomplishment, but to examine whether it has quietly strengthened the thought, “I alone am great.” The seeker must look beyond achievements and ask how knowledge has changed the quality of their conduct.

When Possessiveness Looks Like Love

Human relationships are natural, yet Dr. Newton distinguishes loving another person from attempting to control that person's freedom. He uses marriage as an example: trying to reshape a partner into one's preferred image can turn a relationship into a form of bondage. Sharing a life and accepting responsibilities is one thing; claiming ownership over another person's personality and purpose is quite another.

The quality he associates with genuine love is acceptance. Respecting another person's past, present, and future; allowing them to be themselves; and supporting their life's purpose bring a relationship closer to unconditional love. When two people protect one another's freedom while cooperating in daily life, the relationship becomes a foundation for growth rather than confinement.

Attachment to Possessions, People, and Memories

Possessiveness is not confined to relationships. Money, gold, houses, and property can all serve practical purposes. The difficulty begins when we believe we are incomplete without them. The degree of distress we feel at their loss can reveal the inner hold they have acquired over us.

The speaker also invites us to move beyond narrow divisions such as 'my relatives' and 'my friends' toward a wider sense of human kinship. Repeatedly clinging to painful events from the past is another form of attachment. We can remember the past without continuously carrying its hurt into the present. The invitation is not to abandon responsibility, but to live responsibly while cultivating inward freedom.

Contentment Amid an Endless Chain of Desires

Once we acquire a house, we may want another; once we earn some money, we may seek more. Why, the speaker asks, does desire continue even after our needs have been met? However many houses we own, we can occupy only one at a time. His example of a simple place sufficient for spiritual practice points to the difference between comfort and contentment.

This is not a rejection of material necessities. Rather, it is an invitation to use what we have without allowing the endless demand for more to determine our peace. Recognizing a desire, examining the dissatisfaction beneath it, and distinguishing genuine need from attachment all belong to the work of inner purification.

The Subtle Message in Raktabija's Story

In the myth, each drop of Raktabija's blood that reaches the earth produces another Raktabija. Dr. Newton reads this as an image of ego's capacity to multiply. A subtle sense of 'I' may generate many reactions: one fulfilled desire leads to another, one form of recognition to a demand for more, and mental tendencies reinforce themselves.

He recalls the image of Kali preventing the blood from touching the ground. Within this interpretation, the story suggests noticing a tendency with awareness before it spreads into further reactions. The speaker also mentions simha kriya, the Vishuddha chakra, and obstructions of energy in the context of spiritual practice. These are presented as elements of his spiritual explanation, not as independently established medical claims.

Kaushiki Mahasaraswati: What Should We Seek?

The speaker describes Devi Shakti, within his spiritual framework, as capable of granting both worldly enjoyments and liberating wisdom. Yet the more important question is what we choose to ask for. Is the fulfillment of every desire our highest aim, or should we understand the ignorance that continually produces desires? He connects this inquiry to the wisdom aspect of Mahasaraswati and Kaushiki Mata during Navratri's final three days.

Using the sword of wisdom means seeing our inner bondage as an opportunity for self-inquiry rather than projecting it onto external enemies. Even an attachment that feels pleasant deserves examination if it limits freedom.

Avadhutas: Outer Appearance and Inner State

To explain why spiritual depth cannot be measured by clothing, wealth, or social standing, Dr. Newton speaks of avadhutas. He refers to Venkayya Swami, associated with the Nellore region, and reflects on how people sometimes dismiss an unconventional way of life as madness. What appears incomprehensible to ordinary social expectations may be understood differently within a life of intense devotion.

He also recalls his acquaintance with Chandrasekhar Sharma, whom he describes as a deeply devoted meditator. Someone who lives simply, perhaps even beside a road, cannot be judged by appearances alone. The unusual experiences he recounts belong to the speaker's personal recollections and spiritual descriptions.

The central lesson is to resist hasty judgments based on outward appearance. Humility, careful observation, and inner maturity matter more than visible status.

Nachiketa's Question: What Is Eternal?

Through the story of Nachiketa, the speaker turns toward liberating knowledge. The young seeker meets Yama, the lord of death, and refuses to be distracted by offers of wealth, comfort, and long life. He remains intent on understanding death and the nature of the Self. The story centers on discernment between what passes away and what is enduring.

Dr. Newton relates the teaching of the imperishable Self to Nachiketa Vidya and shares his spiritual understanding of the gross, subtle, and causal dimensions of existence. These are his interpretations of the Upanishadic story. At the heart of Nachiketa's inquiry lies the decision to value self-knowledge above temporary pleasures.

Inner Wounds: The Meaning We Give an Event

Toward the end of the teaching, the speaker asks listeners to examine how ego, possessiveness, fear, and emptiness remain active within us. People do not necessarily respond in identical ways to similar circumstances. For example, children who spend time away from their parents do not all develop the same emotional wound. How an experience is understood and what meaning a person assigns to it also matter.

He suggests that a childhood feeling of restricted freedom may later appear as an intense search for freedom, while the feeling of having gone unrecognized may manifest as a continuing need for approval. These are illustrative invitations to self-examination, not universal psychological diagnoses.

What words hurt us, and why? Why does one remark provoke a disproportionate reaction? Why might the absence of others' recognition leave us feeling empty? Honest attention to such questions can reveal bonds we did not know we were carrying.

The Sword of Wisdom Is Self-Inquiry

On Navratri's seventh day, the destruction of asuras emerges not simply as an external battle but as an invitation to recognize recurring movements of the mind. Shumbha and Nishumbha's attraction and aversion, Raktabija's multiplying ego, the grip of possessiveness, and the dissatisfaction of endless desire all become matters for direct observation in daily life.

Meditation, awareness, trust, surrender, and discernment are the means Dr. Newton emphasizes. The true test of spiritual learning is whether what we understand begins to change how we live.
