The Hidden Inner Meaning in Puranic Stories

In the Jnanodayam Sri Ganesha series, the spiritual essence behind the stories of Ganesha’s avatars was explored based on the Mudgala Purana. Lata’s teaching brought forward the viewpoint that one should not merely listen to the Purana as a story, but understand and apply its tattva to one’s own life.

The Eight Avatars — Inner Transformation of Qualities

The eight avatars of Ganesha were connected with the transformation of tendencies within us such as jealousy (irshya), pride (mada), delusion (moha), greed (lobha), anger (krodha), desire (kama), possessiveness (mamakara), and ego (ahankara). It was explained that in place of jealousy arises mudita (sympathetic joy), in place of pride arises humility, in place of delusion arises discrimination (viveka), and in place of greed arise contentment and generosity. The main message of this teaching is that possessiveness transforms into universal love and ego into equanimity.

Gajanana Avatar — From Greed to Generosity

Through the Gajanana story in the Mudgala Purana, the tale of Lobhasura was explained. In this, the Vinayaka principle is revealed not as one that hates and destroys the demonic quality, but as a compassionate form that transforms it and raises it into a higher quality. It was specially stated that through connection with Gajanana, greed is removed and the quality of generosity arises.

Saguna–Nirguna Parabrahma Swarupa

The form of Gajanana was clarified not merely as a deity with an elephant face, but as the Parabrahma principle that indicates both saguna and nirguna. It was said that by grasping the spiritual meaning behind the form, knowledge arises of what we must let go.

The Mouse Vehicle — Mind, and the Sachchidananda Swarupa

The mouse was explained as a symbol of the mind that turns attention away from our natural satya (truth), jnana (knowledge), and ananda (bliss). The form of Ganesha indicates the path of increasing awareness of the mind that, unknowingly, makes us forget our true nature, and of recognizing that natural swarupa again.

Meditation, Puja, and Connection

Ganapati tattva was explained in connection with iccha shakti, jnana shakti, kriya shakti, and the muladhara state. It was conveyed that the Parabrahma principle should be connected with experientially, whether through puja or through meditation, and that this practice leads in the direction of transforming the greed within us into generosity.

Vighnaraja: Not Merely the Remover of Obstacles, but the One Who Awakens Through Them

We usually worship Vighnaraja as Ganapati who removes obstacles. But in this teaching another dimension of Vighnaraja tattva was introduced. Just as a speed breaker on the road reduces our speed and prevents a major accident, certain obstacles in life create an opportunity to examine our behavior and come out of worldly delusion. When we are moving forward with ego and excessive attachment, a vighna can stop us and become a reminder that turns us toward spiritual reflection. Therefore it was suggested that instead of immediately rejecting every obstacle, we should see with awareness the lesson that is to be learned from it.

The Story of Diti: Tapas, Desire, and Possessiveness

While explaining the Puranic story of Kashyapa Maharshi, Diti, and Indra, it was said how the mind can waver even when intense sadhana is being performed, if possessiveness toward the result increases. The story of Diti performing tapas with attachment and desire for her progeny was shown as an example of the cycle of karma and mamakara. Through this story the point was brought forward that along with the outer discipline of sadhana, the intention behind it and the inner state are also important.

The Birth of Mamasura: Symbol of the ‘I and Mine’ Feeling

Deepti explained the Puranic story of the origin of Mamasura from the pride that arose in Parvati Devi’s tapas and her reunion with Mahadeva. Mamasura is not merely the name of an external demon, but should be seen as a symbol of the mamakara, ahankara, and sense of ownership that can exist in every person. When thoughts such as ‘I achieved it,’ ‘this is mine,’ ‘I want everything’ increase, delusion also increases. Then there is the danger of even changing dharma and adharma to suit one’s own purpose, as explained through names in the story such as Mohini, Dharma–Adharma, and Chintanashana.

Vighnaraja’s Lotus: A Pause for Pride

When Mamasura, with the power of boons, conquered the worlds and even reached the level of binding the gods, Vighnaraja appears. In this story it was explained that he removes Mamasura’s pride not with an arrow but with a lotus. After the ego is removed, Mamasura realizes his mistake and asks for forgiveness. Ganesha forgives him and orders him to support those who walk on the path of dharma. This incident was explained as a spiritual message in which awakening, transformation, and forgiveness are primary rather than punishment.

Spiritual Ego Is Also an Obstacle

The teaching warns that pride can arise not only regarding worldly wealth, family, and achievements, but also regarding the progress made in meditation or jnana sadhana. Spiritual ahankara such as ‘I alone have done more sadhana,’ ‘no one has more knowledge than me’ can block the seeker’s forward step. As Dr. Newton has explained, the movement from ahankara and mamakara toward Group Consciousness was mentioned here. It was said that by listening to the experiences of other seekers and mutually sharing knowledge, one can understand one’s own state more clearly.

Ganapati — Lord of the Ganas, Symbol of Unity Consciousness

Explaining that ‘gana’ means group and ‘Ganapati’ means the lord of the ganas, it was said that the deep message in the Vighnaraja avatar is to go beyond the individual ‘I and mine’ feeling and connect with social cooperation and unity consciousness. When we are unable to overcome our habits and possessiveness alone, the company of good people, group meditation, and sharing of experiences place us back on the path of sadhana. It was suggested that the three fundamental principles of meditation, swadhyaya, and satsanga should be kept in practice.

Application in Life

After listening to this teaching, it was suggested in the discussion that we should recall the obstacles we have faced in the past and examine what we learned from them. Spiritual awareness can be increased by observing when the ‘I and mine’ feeling strengthens within us, by studying knowledge ourselves without blindly believing, by examining with the inner Self, and by remaining in the company of good people. This Jnanodayam reminds us that worshipping Vighnaraja does not mean merely wishing that obstacles disappear, but receiving the lessons they offer and moving forward with dharma, humility, and unity.

The Story of Matsarasura — A Symbol of Envy

In the Vakratunda avatar story, Matsarasura is the main character. While explaining his birth, growth, power of boons, and expansion of authority in the Purana, the speaker brought forward the idea that matsara means envy or jealousy. The dissatisfaction that arises when someone else receives better recognition, wealth, or spiritual experience than ourselves can gradually turn into ego. It was suggested that the asura in the story should be seen not only as an external enemy, but as a symbol of a quality that sometimes strengthens within us.

From Pride and Envy to Attachment Toward External Objects

The family story of Matsarasura was connected with the concept of the arishadvargas. Mada means pride; matsarya means envy. When these two combine, the urge increases to prove to others one’s form, possessions, status, education, victories, service rendered, or the classes one has taught. The names Sundarapriya and Vishayapriya in the story were shown as symbols of a mentality excessively attracted to beauty and external objects. Recognizing that all these exist within us is the first step in reducing their influence.

Vakratunda — A Flexible Mind Not Bound to One Path

‘Vakra’ means curved, ‘tunda’ means trunk. The elephant’s trunk bends and moves according to the situation. Taking this as an example, it was explained that flexibility in thought is needed instead of the rigid attitude ‘the path I thought of is the only right one.’ In spiritual practice there is no need to insist that the same method must suit everyone; what matters is whether any path helps us know our true nature. The speaker shared the example that even while personally practicing anapanasati meditation, others should be given the freedom to continue the practice that suits them.

The Lion Vehicle — Courage and Inner Strength

The meaning behind Vakratunda coming on a lion in this avatar was also explained. The lion is a symbol of courage, strength, and fearlessness. To face qualities such as envy and ego, along with flexibility in the mind, courage and inner strength are needed. It was reminded that merely because a negative feeling appears within us, we need not conclude that we are completely bad; the feeling can be observed with awareness and changed.

Vakratunda Who Stands on the Side of Dharma

When Matsarasura defeated the gods, they take refuge in Ganesha. Vakratunda comes forward to protect dharma. The speaker specially discussed the question Matsarasura asked: ‘If gods and asuras are parts of the same creation, why do you stand on one side?’ The theme of the story in which Vakratunda answers that dharma is the restoration of balance when adharma exceeds limits was explained. Seeing his divine form, Matsarasura realizes his mistake and asks for forgiveness. The main message here is to recognize the negative quality, give it its proper place, and live according to dharma.

Siddhi–Buddhi, Pramoda–Amoda — The Divine Powers Within Us

Siddhi and Buddhi mentioned in the story were explained as indicators of inner perfection and knowledge. Pramoda and Amoda were connected with joy, happiness, and contentment. It was said that when negative thoughts such as envy strengthen, it appears as though the good qualities within us have disappeared, yet they are not completely destroyed. When we again remember our sadhana, knowledge, and joy, strength is added to the divine qualities.

Brahmapriya, Sarvaga, Sarvavit — Beyond External Recognition

Envy can arise when we compare ourselves with others’ praise, spiritual experiences, or victories. At such a time we should remember that our goal is not external recognition but Brahma Jnana. Brahmapriya in the story was explained as love toward Atma Jnana, Sarvaga as the consciousness that pervades everywhere, and Sarvavit as an indicator of complete knowledge. It was said that when our attention turns from external comparisons to inner knowledge, the influence of envy decreases.

Continuous Practice — Transformation of Qualities with Awareness

This discourse reminds us that merely hearing knowledge once does not permanently remove envy and ego. It was explained that it is important to observe the reaction that arises in us on seeing another’s success, to recognize the good qualities we already have, and to cultivate joy and contentment. Negative feelings should not be hated; they should be understood, and with awareness we should decide which qualities to strengthen. This transformation becomes stable only through continuing meditation, swadhyaya, and sadhana.

Referring to the Vedic statement ‘Ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti,’ it was explained that the all-pervading consciousness can also be recognized in the form of Vinayaka. It was suggested that before celebrating a festival it is important to know its spiritual significance.

Vinayaka, Gajamukha, and the Mouse Vehicle

The name ‘Vinayaka’ was explained as the supreme principle beyond whom there is no leader, and also as the power that develops self-discipline (atma shikshana) within us. The elephant-faced form was applied as a symbol of the state that has gone beyond ego, and the mouse as a symbol of the nature of possessiveness — hiding, hoarding, and holding on to ‘mine.’ Making the mouse one’s vehicle means not becoming a slave to possessiveness but gaining mastery over it.

Vakratunda, Ganapati, Vighneshwara

The curved trunk was explained as the form of Pranava, connected with breath practice, and also as a symbol of the power that corrects crooked intellect, crooked path, and the obstacles in life. In the name ‘Ganapati’ the inner meaning of knowledge and bliss was shown. It was said that the obstacles encountered in life sometimes become the cause for self-inquiry and for entering the path of meditation, and Vighneshwara tattva was analyzed extensively.

Siddhi–Buddhi, Labha–Kshema, and Ganadhipatya

The names Siddhi and Buddhi were connected with the spiritual capacity, discrimination (viveka), and Buddha-like state that blossom in the seeker. The names Labha and Kshema were explained with the meanings of obtaining what is to be obtained and protecting what has been obtained. Saying that ‘gana’ means group, it was explained that Ganapati is the symbol of the consciousness that functions in all groups, from the groups of cells in the body to living beings.

Inner Meaning of Chandra Darshana and the Syamantaka Mani Stories

On the occasion of Vinayaka Chavithi the custom of not looking at the moon, the accusations of Nilapani, and the Syamantaka Mani stories related to Sri Krishna were mentioned. It was suggested that these should not be taken merely as external events, but that the spiritual symbols in them should be examined. Aspects such as the qualification required to wear the Syamantaka Mani and conquering principles in the battle with Jambavan were applied to the seeker’s inner journey.

Atma Pradakshina and Muladhara Tattva

Ganesha performing pradakshina around Shiva and Parvati was explained as Atma pradakshina — seeing the divine consciousness within us. There was discussion about Ganapati tattva in the muladhara, the awakening of Kundalini Shakti, and the qualification required for sadhana. The message was given that traveling inward, purifying the mind through meditation, and grasping the inner meaning of the Puranas is the true festival practice.

Vinayaka Chavithi — A Festival of Knowledge in Practice

At the conclusion of the teaching it was suggested that Vinayaka Chavithi should be celebrated meaningfully through meditation, listening to knowledge, and self-inquiry, not limited only to outer festivities. The idea was mainly established that knowing Ganesha tattva means traveling in the direction of awakening the knowledge, bliss, humility, and self-control within us.

Reading the Puranic Story as a Teaching of Principles

Recalling the context of this series of discourses on Ganesha Tattva, Latha Mahendra gives importance to the Mudgala Purana and to the efforts of Ganesha Yogendra Maharaj who made it accessible to all. She suggests that one should not stop at merely viewing the names, forms, and events in the Puranic stories as wonders, but should examine the message they carry relating to the human interior. She explains that the same Ganesha Tattva being narrated through various avatars also serves as a means to understand the different tendencies within us.

Rather than remaining stuck in the discussion of which deity is greater by comparing one form with another, the discourse states that it is important to grasp the principle being taught through that form. Using the example of an actor who assumes different roles, it is explained that even though forms change, the consciousness that is their basis can be recognized.

Nirguna and Saguna — Two Perspectives of the Same Principle

The speaker explains that the Nirguna principle, which is described as being without form, name, and attributes, is worshipped in Saguna forms so that it becomes understandable to the devotee. Ganapati is not merely a specific image; the intention is that through that form a universal principle can be brought closer to the mind. In this background, while also referring to the ideas in the Ganesha Upanishad, the perspective of seeing Ganapati as the principle underlying creation, sustenance, and dissolution is conveyed.

Even in our lives, the same person performs many roles. With the analogy that differences between roles do not mean the person's inner nature is different, she suggests that the diversity of Ganapati avatars should be understood in the same way. The message that stands primarily here is that devotion should not remain limited only to the form, but should lead to the expansive consciousness that the form indicates.

The Entry of Greed in the Gajanana Story

Explaining the story related to the Gajanana avatar, the speaker describes how the tendency of Lobhasura grows. What appears initially as a desire, if it does not stop even after being fulfilled, leads to more desires. She explains that lack of contentment even after obtaining power, wealth, and authority is the characteristic of greed. Referring to stages in the story such as the guidance of Shukracharya, worship, and obtaining boons, she reminds that the most important question is in which direction we use the power we have obtained.

She says that the asura in the Purana is not merely a character existing somewhere outside, but can also be seen as the form of selfish thoughts within us. If the urge to accumulate beyond necessity grows, the joys and sorrows of others may cease to be visible. This story reminds us that there is then a danger that even the power obtained through sadhana may be used not for the expansive good but for personal dominance.

When Craving Joins Greed

The mind running toward another desire as soon as one desire is fulfilled is a commonly experienced matter. In the discourse, the joining of craving (trishna) with greed is specially discussed. Here, craving means not the natural effort to fulfill a need, but the endless thirst that no matter how much comes it is never enough. She explains that the cycle of wanting one more after acquiring one, and wanting more recognition after reaching one level, constantly turns the mind outward.

If this cycle is not observed, happiness always depends on some future achievement. The capacity to enjoy what is already present diminishes. The discourse suggests that while greed limits us, gratitude expands our vision. The practical idea that appears here is that rather than suppressing craving, it is important to see its movement with awareness.

From Envy Toward Mudita

Referring to situations such as feeling that we too should have what others have, and the mind becoming disturbed by others' success, the nature of envy is explained. She says that at the root of envy there may be the narrow feeling that 'priority should come only to me.' Grasping the Ganapati principle means recognizing that narrow feeling and going beyond it.

In contrast, the speaker introduces the feeling called Mudita. Mudita is being able to truly rejoice when good happens to another. It is explained that when others' happiness does not appear as competition to us, and we too become partners in that joy, love expands. The message that the small circle of 'my people' can expand from the family to society, to other beings, and to the universe is important in this part.

The Name Gajanana — A Pointer Beyond Form

Latha Mahendra says that the name Gajanana should not be seen only with the external meaning of elephant face. When speaking about the principle that existed before creation, if one holds only to the physical form and questions, the essential meaning may not be reached. The spiritual pointer conveyed through name and form should be examined; both the literal meaning and the symbolic meaning should be given their appropriate place.

She reminds that in the commentarial tradition there is an inner meaning for every aspect of the Ganapati form. However, they are not explanations about physical structure; they are symbolic teachings related to the mind, consciousness, and sadhana. Seen in that way, the name Gajanana becomes a means to awaken an expansive vision within us.

The Mouse Vehicle — Symbol of the Restless Mind

The discourse also specially discusses Ganapati's vehicle, the mouse. Showing analogies with the mouse's characteristics of hiding things and nibbling them away, she explains that the mind too runs after many things without our knowing. She asks us to observe how small attractions snatch away our attention, time, and peace.

Ganapati being seated on the vehicle is not hating those tendencies; she presents it as a metaphor indicating the state of being able to guide them with awareness. Observing where the mind is running, and bringing it back to a meaningful resolve, become part of the practice.

Karma, Resolve, and Self-Examination

The speaker examines the relationship between desires, actions, and their results from the perspective of karma. She says that rather than immediately giving a simple judgment as to why some experiences are arising, one should examine one's own responses. Instead of remaining stuck only in remembering the past and suffering, what matters is with what kind of resolve we are living now.

This teaching suggests that through practices such as meditation, self-study, gratitude, and rejoicing in the joy of others, one can journey from greed toward expansive love. When the Gajanana story is concluded, the principal question that remains is: is the restlessness that we need more increasing, or is the expansiveness of sharing what we have increasing?

Puranic Background of the Dhumravarna Story

The speaker introduces how the Dhumravarna avatar is told in the Mudgala Purana in the form of dialogues. Through names such as Saunaka, Suta Maharshi, Daksha, and Mudgala Maharshi, the tradition by which the story passed from one person to another is recalled. Referring to the conversation that took place between Shiva and Vishnu, it is explained that the Ganapati avatar is not merely a story of the destruction of a demon, but a tattva story that reveals the nature of ahankara.

The speaker’s method is to connect the wonderful events in the story with present-day life when listening to them. Through the Purana saying that even the gods can develop ahankara, the message is brought forward that no one should think they are completely beyond this tendency.

From the Subtle Ahankara of the Sun to the Birth of Ahankarasura

The story proceeds that in the management of creation Brahma entrusts a part of his responsibilities to the Sun. Along with that responsibility a slight feeling of “I alone am doing it” arises in the Sun. Bhanumurti explains the Puranic episode that from the sneeze that came at that time Ahankarasura was born. He sees the asura who began small quickly becoming big as an indication that ahankara begins as a small thought and can strengthen rapidly.

When we receive a responsibility, success, or recognition we can accept it as service; or we can turn it into self-importance with “this became possible only because of me.” The difference between these two viewpoints is subtle, yet the result is large, he says. Recognizing the birth of ahankara is the first step in working with it.

Guidance of Shukracharya and the Boon of Ganesha

Shukracharya stands as guru to Ahankarasura and suggests that he perform upasana of Ganesha. After long tapas Ganesha becomes pleased, and Ahankarasura asks for the boon that death should not come through beings covered by maya or possessing blood and flesh. The determination he showed in this stage of acquiring power is remarkable; yet the direction in which the purpose of that power turned after obtaining it is a crucial turning point in the story.

In this context the speaker explains that tapas, discipline, and power do not by themselves produce humility. Even if capacity increases through sadhana, without inner inquiry that capacity itself can become the basis for a sense of domination. Therefore he suggests that we should frequently look at what our intention is.

Vishayapriya City, Mamata, Garva, and Shreshtha

After obtaining the boon the story says that Ahankarasura established a city called Vishayapriya, married Mamata, and obtained sons named Garva and Shreshtha. Bhanumurti specially analyzes these names. He interprets Vishayapriya as attraction toward sense objects; Mamata as the insistence of “mine”; Garva as self-praise; and Shreshtha as the feeling “there is no one greater than me.”

He explains that these tendencies do not exist one by one, but mutually strengthen one another like links in a chain. When the healthy state of “I know something” changes into the closed state of “I alone know everything,” it becomes difficult to listen to others’ words. The role of companions who encourage adharma is also connected with this context in the story.

Domination over the Three Worlds — Expansion of Ahankara

Ahankarasura’s desires do not stop with his city. In the attempt to gain authority over the three worlds he subjects the gods and other beings to fear. Because of the effect of Ganesha’s boon it is not possible to confront him, and the gods discuss among themselves. Finally they decide that they must pray to the same Ganesha who gave the boon.

Bhanumurti reminds us that the ahankara within us also does not stop with one victory. It expands in forms such as wanting everyone to accept our opinion, looking down on others, and trying to control even relationships. This story shows that even while outer success increases, inner peace may decrease.

Appearance of Dhumravarna and Narada’s Peace Message

The story proceeds that Ganesha responds to the gods’ prayer and appears in the form of Dhumravarna. Even before the war he sends Narada as messenger and gives the message that Ahankarasura should stop his evil deeds and come for peace. Even though warning signs appear to Ahankarasura, with pride in his power he rejects that opportunity.

The speaker compares this episode with the warnings that come in our life. Sometimes others’ words, a failure, or a problem in a relationship may remind us to correct our behavior. If we can listen to those indications immediately we can prevent a larger conflict. However, when ahankara is strong, even the advice we receive may appear as an insult.

War, Loss, and Returning to the Guru

After rejecting peace the war begins. Before Dhumravarna’s power Ahankarasura’s army cannot stand; his sons also perish. With all his supports collapsing, the frightened Ahankarasura returns to Shukracharya. He admits that he did not heed the indication that had come earlier and that he does not know what to do now.

Then the guru tells him that there is no other way except sharanagati. Here sharanagati is not merely accepting defeat; it is the state of being able to see the loss that occurred because of one’s ahankara. The speaker explains that the main transformation in the story is stopping the covering up of one’s mistake and becoming ready for change.

Ahankara Melting in the Presence of Ganesha

With the darshana of Ganesha the pride in Ahankarasura decreases, and he praises him and seeks refuge. Even though Dhumravarna forgives him, he reminds him of the harm done in the past and warns him not to increase that tendency henceforth. In this episode priority is given to transformation rather than punishment.

Knowing that there is ahankara in us is one stage; accepting the pain caused to others because of it is a deeper stage. Bhanumurti says, based on this story, that true change appears only through asking for forgiveness, listening, and changing behavior.

Smoke, Jnana Agni, Jnana Khadga — Symbols of Dhumravarna

Dhumra means smoke. Just as there is fire behind smoke, the speaker symbolically explains that jnana agni is needed behind inner purification. Our old habits and concepts covered by maya come into the light through knowledge. It is suggested that the form of Dhumravarna should be seen not merely as a fearsome form, but as the power of transformation that removes ahankara.

In the context of Kaliyuga he also mentions symbols such as the blue horse and the jnana khadga. That sword is explained as a symbol of knowledge that pierces ignorance and maya, not for conquering outsiders. These explanations are the tattvarthas given by the speaker; the sense appears in his words that there is no need to read them only as physical events.

Meditation, Swadhyaya, and the Question “Who Am I?”

Bhanumurti says that merely listening to the story is not enough to reduce ahankara. While doing meditation one should continue swadhyaya; one should observe how our reactions, judgments, and comparisons are changing. He warns that deciding by comparing experiences arising in meditation with others — “I got more” or “I didn’t get it” — can lead to another kind of ahankara.

He suggests that questions such as “Who am I?” and “What is this situation teaching me?” should be made part of life inquiry. Seeing Ganesha first in the temple is also explained as a symbol of leaving ahankara there and entering within. The essence of the story: recognizing the ahankara within us and living with humility is more important than conquering Ahankarasura outside.

Birth of Krodhasura — When Anger Becomes a Tendency

Harika Suri explains how Krodhasura was born in the story related to the Lambodara avatar. Even though anger first appears as a reaction, if it is repeatedly nourished it becomes a power that influences our thoughts and decisions.

She suggests that the story of Krodhasura should be compared with the impulse within us. When anger comes, our own words appear correct to us; the space to understand the other person’s situation decreases. Is Sankalpa for Selfishness or for Collective Good?

She reminds us that when power, opportunity, and knowledge come to us we can shape them so that they are useful to others. Why Does the Past Good Not Appear in Anger?

This matter appears clearly in family and in friendships. If one word is disliked it may feel as though the entire relationship should be rejected. That is why her teaching suggests that opportunity should be given to awareness before taking decisions at a time when emotion is heightened.

Symbols of Krodha, Priti, and Family Relationships

In the story of Krodhasura the speaker also examines the names of other characters. Especially she mentions how feelings that appear opposite, such as krodha and priti, exist together in the same life. We may love someone and yet become angry with them; impulse may appear more in close relationships.

Therefore merely because anger has come we cannot conclude that there is no love; likewise we cannot justify the harm done with anger in the name of love being present. What is needed is to observe honestly the feelings that are working in us at the same time. She explains that by seeing the Puranic characters as symbols of these mental conflicts the story comes closer to present-day life.

The Peak of Anger, the Fall of Impulse

Harika Suri explains in the context of the story that when any tendency reaches its peak its change begins. When anger increases we may speak harsh words, hurt those close to us, and later feel regret. Only after the impulse decreases does the effect of the actions we have done become clearly visible.

From her teaching it is understood that merely suppressing anger is not the solution; observing it while it is in the stage of increasing is important. If we are able to ask “In what state is my mind now?” we can see that impulse does not completely gain mastery over our actions.

Anger Melts Before Lambodara’s Vishvarupa

In the story the vision of Lambodara’s vishvarupa comes as a crucial episode. The speaker explains that when Krodhasura, who considered himself very great, comes to know how small the creation he knew is, his ahankara and impulse decrease. Referring to the vastness of many brahmandas, she conveys the message that a person should not regard his small range as the whole truth.

When we regard our problem as the whole world it appears unbearable. When seen with a vast vision the same problem finds a different place. She explains that this universal vision is not rejecting pain; it is a spiritual perspective that helps change our response to be more balanced.

Satya Darshana and Coming Out of Dualities

The speaker says that when the mind is caught in the duality of “I am right, the other is wrong,” conflict continues. When truth is grasped more deeply it is known that the opinions we hold are not complete. Krodhasura’s transformation is connected with this understanding.

Satya darshana does not mean merely seeing a wonderful form; it also means recognizing how ahankara, limited vision, and impulse are working within us. Then the readiness to change our response increases more than the insistence on changing others. This stands as the practical lesson in the story.

From the Background of Jalandhara to the Story of Kamasura

She examines every stage in the story with this question: Are we driving the desire, or is the desire driving us?

From Desire to Endless Trishna

It is natural for a person to have needs. Harika Suri recognizes desires such as earning for livelihood, taking care of the family, and striving for good opportunities as ordinary parts of life. However, she explains that if, as soon as one goal is reached, the restlessness that another goal is necessarily needed begins, then contentment is postponed forever.

She gives the example that we should examine where the need ends and endless hoarding begins — earn for the children, then for their future, then for the grandchildren. Her intention is not to find fault with earning or planning; the main thing is to ask the question whether we have tied our entire peace to a result in the future.

Kamasura’s Sadhana, Obtaining the Boon, and the Bond with Trishna

In the story of Kamasura the speaker explains episodes such as upasana, acquiring power, and obtaining the boon. Even after the desire is fulfilled his aspiration does not stop. Mentioning his relationship with the character Trishna, she comments that when endless thirst is added to desire it becomes stronger.

Here Trishna appears not merely as a Puranic character but as a symbol of the inner pressure of “still more is needed.” If that pressure increases, the difference of “mine–yours,” comparisons, and the sense of domination may strengthen. She says that when the influence of desires on the mind and intellect increases, it becomes difficult to recognize what we really need.

Fulfilling Desire and Becoming a Slave to Desire

Wanting an object and striving to obtain it is one state. Feeling that life is wasted if it is not obtained is another state. Harika Suri clearly explains the difference between these two. To understand desire one must see how much space it occupies in our thoughts and what kind of decisions we are taking for its sake.

Mentioning the context of Vishvamitra, Vashishtha Maharshi, and Kamadhenu, she explains how the insistence on necessarily obtaining what others have increases on seeing it. One may try to fulfill a desire; but if that effort destroys our values, relationships, and peace, there is a need to examine it again, she suggests.

Family Life and Spiritual Practice — Place for Both

The speaker specially reminds that when interest in spiritual practice increases, family responsibilities should not be neglected. Even though activities such as meditation, study, and service are valuable, giving time to those who live with us is also part of life. She says not to turn spirituality into a way of escaping from the family.

Just as the time allotted for sadhana should be respected, the time for talking with family members and the time for listening to their needs should also be respected. In her teaching it appears that there is no need to completely leave one field for growth in another field; balance can be achieved with awareness.

Worship of Mayuresha and the Appearance of Vikata

In the story the suggestion comes that Mayuresha should be worshipped for the solution of the problems caused by Kamasura. Mentioning places such as Dandakaranya and Bhusvananda, Harika Suri explains the background related to Ganapati worship. Thereafter she introduces the episode in which Mayuresha appears in the form of Vikata.

She says that one should not stop only with the negative notions that usually come to the mind for the word “Vikata.” This form is explained as a symbol of the tattva that understands desires and removes their domination. The avatar in the story is closer to the question of how dissatisfaction and delusion within dissolve than to a display of power over outsiders.

The Peacock Vehicle — Acceptance, but Not Clinging

The speaker specially analyzes Vikata’s peacock vehicle. Comparing the peacock’s qualities such as beauty and attraction with the world of desires, she explains that completely rejecting them is not spirituality. Using it as a vehicle means driving them; not tying one’s identity with them — she states this symbolic meaning.

In our life there may be objects, relationships, and successes. They can be used and enjoyed. But one must come out of the state that if they are not there our inner peace completely collapses. This metaphor reminds us of the difference between acceptance and clinging.

Kamasura’s Power Decreasing Before Vikata

Harika Suri explains that in the story when the form of Vikata appears, Kamasura reaches a state in which he cannot use his power. For him who considered himself invincible, knowing his limitation is an important turn in the story. This episode indicates how temporary it is to think that with the notion that power exists we can control everything.

When the mind that runs behind desires stops for a moment and looks at itself, opportunity arises for new understanding. That is the beginning of self-inquiry. The speaker’s teaching suggests that change becomes possible by recognizing what kind of insistence desire is creating within us, without seeing desire as an enemy.

The Desire to Change Others Is Also Desire

In the final part of the discourse Harika Suri examines the strong desire that people we like should change some habits. Even if our intention is their welfare, if we insist that they must change exactly according to our words, that too can become a kind of desire for control, she suggests.

Giving information to others, helping, and showing a good path can be done. However, space should also be given to their journey and their decisions. She explains that observing how our expectations are working, rather than becoming angry that they did not change in the way we hoped, is part of spiritual maturity.

Awareness of Desires in Daily Life

This discourse invites us that when a desire arises in the mind, without immediately judging it as good or bad, we should examine “Is this a real need? Is it a habit? Did it come from comparison? Did it come from fear?” With this examination the decision whether to fulfill the desire, postpone it, or leave it can be taken more clearly.

What the Vikata avatar story finally reminds us of is not the command to live a life without desires. It is the question whether it is possible to live without becoming a slave to them while remaining amid desires, responsibilities, and relationships. Meditation, swadhyaya, acceptance, and balance with the family — the speaker shows all these as practical paths to that question.

Is It Possible to Speak with Peace Amid Differences?

While introducing a new nine-day series in the Jnanodayam program, it was recalled at the beginning of the program how the Nonviolent Communication classes held earlier had an impact on the lives of the participants. Differences naturally appear in the family, in society, and throughout the world. Talking to each other again after a quarrel is one matter; truly letting go of the remaining pain, judgments, and misunderstandings in that quarrel is another matter. It was explained that Marshall Rosenberg’s work Speak Peace in a World of Conflict was taken as the basis to examine the difference between these two.

Kiran’s discourse invited us to see this matter not only as theory but as practice. To speak with peace one must first recognize what is happening within us. From the points he explained it is known that the effort to understand our response, rather than the effort to change the other person, can open a new possibility in relationships.

Two Questions That Guided Marshall Rosenberg

Kiran introduced Marshall Rosenberg’s life and the inspirations that led to his research. What is the reason a person wants to hurt another person? What is it that inspires the same person to desire another’s welfare and to help him? It was said that these two questions occupied the main place in Rosenberg’s thoughts.

It was explained that with the interest of knowing the answer to these questions he studied psychology. Thereafter he developed a conversational method useful for resolving conflicts between individuals. The manner in which practices first held with small groups later expanded to more workshops was also mentioned in the discourse. The central idea of this method related to mutual understanding is that it should be useful not merely for winning in argument, but for listening to one person’s pain and another person’s need.

The Example of the Grandmother Who Lived Compassion

Kiran specially mentioned the compassion shown by Rosenberg’s grandmother in his childhood. Once a person in torn clothes and hungry came to their house. Seeing his condition the grandmother invited him inside and helped him to clean himself and obtain food; this incident was explained. This incident was taken not merely as a story related to charity, but as an example of the vision that can see the other person with their human needs rather than with a label.

Later in the questions and answers a participant expressed doubt about the trust needed to take such an unknown person into the house. That doubt reminds us of the delicate relationship that exists among compassion, trust, and caution. The compassionate viewpoint stated in the discourse should be read not as a rule that everyone must necessarily follow the same action, but as an invitation to understand people’s situation without first diminishing them.

Observation Is Different, Judgment Is Different

The first key practice in Nonviolent Communication is being able to state what happened without mixing in judgment. To show this difference Kiran explained the example of one person describing another as ‘lazy.’ ‘She is lazy’ is not the event that happened; it is the label we placed on that event. Details of what the person did, what they did not do, and in what context it happened are closer to observation.

Here the goal is not merely changing words. When we truly believe the label we have placed on someone, it becomes difficult to see their other qualities. When the same thing that happened is stated specifically, space is created for conversation. The remark ‘you are always like this’ may increase argument; mentioning a specific context may help mutual understanding.

Roommate Light Example: What Is Behind the Words?

An important practice that came in the discourse is related to a roommate who keeps the light on in the room at night. First a person expressed dissatisfaction with his roommate. When asked to state it more clearly, the event that ‘at bedtime the roommate keeps the light on without turning it off’ came out. This is not a judgment on the person’s nature; it is a description related to observable behavior.

The next question is more important: How are you feeling at that time? What feelings are arising in you because the light is on? This question takes the conversation from the level of ‘he is doing wrong’ to the level of ‘what is happening in me?’ It is understood that needs such as sleep, rest, or mutual consideration may be related here. However, only the person himself should recognize which need is primary for him; outsiders should not decide by guessing.

Are We Stating a Feeling or Making an Accusation?

Kiran explained that while thinking we are expressing the feelings that have arisen in us, many times we state a judgment on the other person in another form. For example, in the sentence ‘I felt as though I was insulted’ there may also be a meaning placed on the other person’s action along with our inner experience. Therefore the real feeling behind those words should be recognized more carefully.

Recognizing feelings such as anger, pain, disappointment, worry, happiness, and gratitude is a practice. The discourse suggests that rather than dividing feelings into positive and negative and suppressing some, we should see what information they are giving us. Even if the right word is not found immediately, asking ourselves ‘Is this really my feeling, or is it my decision about someone else?’ is the first step.

Met and Unmet Needs Behind Feelings

Why does joy arise in one context and pain in another? How are feelings related to our needs? Kiran expanded these questions. It was explained that along with physical needs such as food, water, rest, and touch, psychological needs such as respect, connection, and understanding are also important in our life. It was mentioned that in spiritual life too there are needs related to our inner experiences.

A small infant experiences its need even though it cannot state it in words. Even after becoming adults the needs do not disappear; but we often lose the capacity to recognize them. There is a possibility that the dissatisfaction within us increases further because of feeling ‘my need has no value.’ The discourse emphasized that recognizing needs is not equal to selfishness; needs are natural for a living person.

While Living for Others Are We Forgetting Our Needs?

Kiran examined some messages learned from childhood from family and society. Even though fulfilling the other person’s needs is good, if the habit forms of regarding stating one’s own needs as selfishness, the connection with our inner self may decrease. In this context it can be recognized that accepting one’s need and forcing another person to fulfill it are not the same.

By recognizing needs clarity arises as to why our feelings are coming. Understanding ‘I need respect’ is different from judging ‘you are bad because you did not respect me.’ This difference may reduce the conflict within us and show a new path for speaking with the other person.

Incident on the Road: One Behavior Is Not the Whole Person

In the questions and answers a participant mentioned the anger that arises on seeing the behavior of other vehicle drivers while traveling on a scooter. The doubt was expressed of how to understand the response changing in such contexts even though there is meditation and spiritual understanding. In response Kiran explained how useful it is to recognize our need.

On seeing a person driving a vehicle dangerously on the road one may generalize ‘everyone in this society is like this.’ But what we saw is only one aspect of that person’s behavior. He may have other good qualities in other contexts. When it is recognized that impatience arose because the need for our safety, orderliness, or mutual consideration was not met, it becomes easier to take appropriate action for the situation without condemning the person wholly, he explained.

Questions and Answers: Compassion, Meditation Awareness, Permission for Feelings

The members’ questions connected the main teaching to various contexts in life. The doubt that arose on the story related to the grandmother’s compassion created an opportunity to examine our vision toward strangers. In another question a participant explained about feeling as though awareness decreased in the middle while going into experiences during meditation. This led to thought on the difference between observing inner experiences and giving them meaning.

Someone else shared the experience of tears suddenly coming on seeing the form of Vinayaka. In that context Kiran importantly said that without immediately going to a decision of why the feeling came, one should recognize that the sorrow or emotion has arisen and give permission to it. Being in connection with the aliveness within us does not mean accepting only joy; it also means being able to observe pain.

The First Practice for Peaceful Conversation

Today’s discourse as a whole indicates a sequence: first observe what happened without judgment; then recognize the feeling that arose in us; then come to know the need behind that feeling. With the same vision, when we listen to the other person we are able to see that there may also be some need behind their behavior. With that one can move from a conversation that proves wrong to a conversation that leads to mutual understanding.

This is not a skill that is completed with one class. Repeated practice is needed in the way we speak with family members, with colleagues, with persons we encounter while traveling, and also with ourselves. As Kiran explained, peace is not only a situation without differences; it is the effort to maintain human connection while recognizing our feelings and needs with awareness even amid differences.

Devi also recalls a shloka from Bhartrihari’s Subhashitas that tells the glory of speech. Ornaments, pearl necklaces, fragrant substances, and beautiful decorations give external beauty to a person. External decoration may attract a person for a moment; but the gentleness and trustworthiness in speech show long-term influence in relationships. This vision that connects a person’s beauty with his speech makes known why the quality of words is important in NVC practice.

Devi says that she first came to know Marshall Rosenberg’s teachings through the previous NVC series in Jnanodayam. She reminds us that in traditional spiritual teachings too there are many indications about speech, compassion, and self-inquiry. She sees as the speciality in Rosenberg’s effort the turning of that knowledge into a method useful for the conflicts in life. This comparison is Devi’s personal spiritual commentary; it should be remembered that she is seeing the life journeys of different gurus with the connection she has experienced.

6. Why Compassion? Why Violence? — An Inquiry

According to Devi’s explanation, from a young age Rosenberg asked himself questions about human behavior. Why do some remain with compassion even in the most difficult situations? Why do others go toward violence? What is the cause of violent behavior? Why are all people not able to remain with compassion? Devi says that for the answer to these questions he went toward psychology and continued higher studies.

She compares this journey with the questioning nature of the Buddha. This comparison with the Buddha’s life story is not to prove historical equality; it was used to indicate the inquiry of wanting to know what is the root of human suffering.

Devi shares honestly that when she began NVC practice it felt very difficult. When someone says something it is easy for anger to come. She says that in the beginning the difference of whether what we are saying is a feeling or an analysis or judgment about the other person may not be known.

After recognizing that feeling another question is faced: ‘What is the unmet need behind this feeling?’ There too the mind may feel like a blank paper, she says. Even though words are ready to throw out at the moment anger comes, words are not found so easily to recognize our need. That is why she compares this practice with the churning of the ocean of milk. 9. Is Our Natural Nature Compassion?

10. From Words That Teach Retaliation Toward New Conversation

Devi mentions the examples of elders encouraging when children quarrel and come home: ‘Why didn’t you hit back?’, ‘You should also say to those who said to you!’ From childhood the idea that counter-attacking itself is strength may have formed in us. Speaking that way may feel like having won, like having maintained our honor. If the same response continues for generations, it is no wonder that it feels natural to us.

However, she suggests that blaming parents or previous generations for these habits is not the solution. They also learned the language and manner of response that came to them. The change she speaks of here is not remaining weak and silent. It is regaining the freedom to respond. Going toward a state in which understanding decides our speech, not impulse.

Devi says that she questioned herself as to how it is if we who are meditating also do not practice this. She obtained in that the inspiration that when Rosenberg was able to work among ordinary people and amid conflicts, those who are doing meditation practice can pay more attention to changing their conversation. Before Changing Words We Should See Our Inner Self

Nirmala Kalepu introduced the topic of the third day in this series proceeding on the basis of the book Speak Peace in a World of Conflict. She explained that the manner of speaking with others is not merely the selection of polite words; change is needed in our thoughts and responses as well. The speaker recalled the four stages of NVC discussed in previous classes: observation, feelings, needs, request. The first step is seeing what happened without judgment. The second is recognizing what feeling that event caused in us. The third is knowing the met or unmet need related to that feeling. Finally the fourth is making a clear request without blaming the other person or forcing them. The discourse suggests that this method is not for controlling others; it is useful for increasing understanding between us and them.

Marshall Rosenberg’s Aspiration: Conversation for Peace

The speaker did not see the work Marshall Rosenberg did merely as teaching a communication method. She explained it as the effort he made for connection to increase in place of violent words among people. It was mentioned in the discourse that the help that came for this service was used for the spread of NVC rather than for personal benefits. When occasions of having spoken with anger in the past come to memory, regret may arise in us. However, the speaker explained that holding on to that memory and punishing ourselves with “How bad am I?” can become another violent inner conversation. She said that what is important is to see how to move forward now with the knowledge that is known.

The speaker shared the viewpoint that people can naturally remain with love and mutual cooperation. She commented that under the influence of society, upbringing, and habits, criticisms, comparisons, and punishments become part of ordinary conversation. This thought should be read not as a historical dating, but as the discourse message of recognizing the compassion within us again. Even if we are unable to change the past, the present response can be changed.

Why a Different Response Each Time to the Same Mistake?

Sharing her experience in the education field, Nirmala did self-inquiry that even if a student makes a big mistake she is able to respond peacefully in some contexts, while in another context there is a possibility of responding intensely even to a small matter. She suggested that here the student’s behavior alone does not decide our anger. Our stress, expectations, and unmet needs at that moment also have an influence.

Therefore without stopping with the simple explanation “anger came to me because he did that,” one should ask oneself “What felt important to me at that time? Which need did I feel was not met?” This question does not give justification to the other person’s action; it opens the path of taking responsibility for our response.

The Mother Who Shouted at the Children: The Need Behind the Anger

As an example told in Rosenberg’s training, the speaker explained a context in which a mother spoke loudly with her daughter. That mother felt pain that she should have said it more gently. However, the discourse examined the manner in which questions were asked so as to know which need was not met for her at that moment, without ending that incident with the judgment “I am a bad mother.”

This example shows how anger increases when the feeling comes that a need was not met. At the same time this does not mean that the mother had no love for her daughter. Love, stress, and impatience can exist in us at the same time. If behavior is to be changed, not merely suppressing anger, but recognizing the need behind it and seeking the opportunity to speak more gently.

From Pain to Discrimination: The Teacher’s Words the Speaker Recalled

Nirmala recalled a sentence written by the physics teacher in the autograph book during her tenth-class days. She saw in those words the idea that pain makes a person think; thought increases discrimination; discrimination can make life more joyful. She connected this memory with the study of NVC.

Here the intention is not to praise pain as great. She explained that when pain arises, instead of abusing ourselves with “Why did I do like this?”, we can see “What is this incident teaching me?” Then space is formed to think how to respond if the same incident is faced again. Recognizing the mistake is necessary; stamping that the mistake itself is our personality is not necessary.

Mistakes Are Not Judgments; They Are Information for Learning

The speaker explained that when those who participated in Rosenberg’s training talk about the mistakes they made, they use self-blame words such as “I am a fool,” “I should not have done that.” She asked to examine whether such words are helping change or pulling us down further. She specially brought forward the idea of Learning without self-hatred.

Taking responsibility on seeing a mistake and hating ourselves are two different things. The first is useful for growth; the second may take our vision away from the solution. Learning with patience, saying apology if needed, and trying to respond newly in the next context — all these are parts of change with compassion. Nirmala also shared the matter that she tells students that when they do good work they should congratulate themselves. That encouragement increases the confidence that we are learning.

In another training example a mother shouted at the children with the worry that she was getting late for a program. Later she severely blamed herself that she was not a good mother. When speaking with her it became clear how important it was to her to reach on time and to respect those organizing the program. It came to light in the discussion that the fear that that need would not be fulfilled itself increased the stress at that time.

The main matter in this example is not justifying her shouting. After recognizing the value of attention toward time management, it is showing that a way of speaking more respectfully with the children can be found. The speaker explained that a person who loves the children can also hurt them under stress; while taking responsibility for that behavior one can come out from self-blame. She also connected her experience of wanting to reach QLU classes on time to this context.

From Self-Compassion to Understanding the Other Person’s Needs

The discourse says that after understanding the stress within us we can also listen to the other person’s needs. Change may not become complete all at once; yet every context can be seen newly.

What Does Speaking After Thinking Mean?

Usually it is said not to respond immediately when in emotion. Even though that suggestion is useful, if it is not known what kind of examination should happen in the mind, that pause alone may not be enough. In the introductory introduction it was explained that the Nonviolent Communication method being learned in this series provides a path for that inner examination. When an event happens, knowing the differences of what we saw, what commentary we made about it, what feeling arose in us, and what need is behind that feeling is the foundation for conversation.

When we immediately understand the word the other person said as ‘I was insulted,’ our response revolves only around that judgment. If we come to know which pain, which fear that same word touched in us, another choice becomes visible to us. Likewise opportunity is also formed to listen to what need is behind the other person’s words. This is not forcibly suppressing anger; it is knowing what is being indicated in us through anger.

Why Do We Hesitate to State Our Needs?

Clearly stating the need that is in the mind is not easy in many contexts. According to the speaker’s explanation, there may be fear that if we express the need others will regard us as selfish. There may also be worry that they will not respond to our word, or fear that they will directly reject it. These make us remain without saying the matter we need. In some contexts we hope that the other person will understand without our stating the need; when that does not happen dissatisfaction increases.

Recognizing these fears does not mean seeing them as wrong. The responses faced earlier when the need was stated may have increased caution in us. Observing such caution, stating our need not as a demand imposed on the other person but as a respectful request is an important practice in NVC. Even if the other person says ‘no’ to the request, there is no need for the conversation to end. The need behind that ‘no’ can also be listened to.

Feelings, Needs, Judgments — Three Different Levels

When pain has arisen in us it is easy to say ‘they are not caring for me.’ But that may be an interpretation we made on a person’s behavior. Behind it there may be loneliness, disappointment, affection, or the need for recognition. If we express only the judgment the other person may try to defend themselves; when we clearly state our feeling and need, space may form for mutual understanding.

In the same way, while listening to others’ words too, without stopping only with the decision ‘they are speaking wrongly,’ it is necessary to know what pain is in their experience. This is not agreeing with their every word. Even though there is disagreement, respecting their human need is possible. This difference is very important in listening with empathy.

Empathy in Marshall Rosenberg’s Story

In the discourse a story related to Marshall Rosenberg’s mediation experience came for mention. The speaker explained that in a meeting he faced severe criticisms because he was an American. Instead of immediately counter-arguing those criticisms, it was said that he tried to listen to the pain caused by war, fear, and insecure conditions in the lives of those speaking angrily.

What is important in this story is not the words used in the criticisms; it is the effort to recognize the pain behind them. When someone is intensely blaming, the ordinary response is defending our intentions or finding fault with them. What the speaker explained as the path Rosenberg showed is only trying to understand what security need, what human suffering is behind their anger. When listened to in that way, the person in the conflict also has the opportunity to state their experience more clearly.

This incident should be seen as a general principle: there is no need to defend against criticism; it is possible to listen to the pain behind the criticism. The place, persons, and historical details related to the story should be further confirmed with the original video before publication.

Empathy Does Not Mean Immediately Giving Advice

When a person close to us states pain, it feels as though a solution should be suggested immediately. Words such as ‘it should be done that way,’ ‘think like this,’ ‘that is not a big problem’ may come with good intention, yet the opportunity for that person to fully state their experience may decrease. The speaker specially mentioned listening with empathy in this context. First giving space to the other person’s words, understanding the feeling they are experiencing, and trying to listen to what their need is are necessary.

When someone is in pain, the attention we give may be more valuable than the answer we say. If the listening person is able to set aside their decisions for a short while, the speaking person may also observe the experience within themselves newly. This listening is not passive; it is a living relationship that remains with attention, patience, and without judgment.

The speaker shared an experience that arose for her with Dr. Newton. She explained that when he listened to her words without judgment and with full attention, even though he did not specially state a solution, a new understanding arose in her. Through this example it was conveyed that empathy is not always giving an answer to our problem from outside.

Sometimes because of the experience that someone is listening to our words peacefully, we ourselves are able to see our situation from a new angle. What we need, what is paining us becomes clear. The speaker used this experience as an example of the effect of empathy. In this appears the suggestion that not only remembering the guru with respect, but also practicing that listening quality in our daily relationships.

How Do Childhood Messages Influence Our Conversation?

The manner of expressing our feelings is not merely a habit formed today. The speaker explained that the messages heard while growing up in the family and in society influence our responses. Messages such as ‘girls should not get angry,’ ‘boys should not cry,’ ‘one should not ask so much’ may stand as obstacles even to recognizing feelings. With the fear that if a feeling is expressed it may not be accepted, we may hide it.

However, by hiding feelings we lose the information they tell about our needs. If we are able to recognize pain we can ask questions of whether we need comfort, respect, rest, or connection. In the same way, without seeing another’s tears only as weakness or anger only as wrong, we can know in what experience that person is. This change begins from small family conversations.

Respect in Request, Freedom in Answer

Stating our need and pressuring another person to listen to our word are not the same. In conversation with empathy, while clearly asking what we need, the other person’s situation should also be respected. If they do not agree, listening to what limitation or need they have at that time is useful rather than deciding them as loveless persons.

This viewpoint is specially needed in family relationships. Just as the desire that our word should be heard is natural, the need that the other person also should have their feelings heard is equally natural. If in the effort to fulfill one person’s need the other person’s need is completely neglected, dissatisfaction remains in the relationship. If both persons’ needs can be recognized in the same conversation, new paths for the problem may become visible.

Even Though There Is Difference, Connection Is Possible

Empathy does not say that everyone should come to the same opinion. Even if our opinions are different, we can understand the matters important to each other. If anger is visible in someone’s words, behind it there may be the need for safety or respect. Even if rejection is visible in another’s silence, behind it there may be the need for time or clarity. These are not matters to be decided beforehand; they are to be known through listening.

That is why empathy is more than a conversation method. It is the effort we make to see the person in front of us not as a ‘problem’ but as a human being with feelings and needs. When such a vision forms we also become ready to express our needs without blames.

Empathy as Daily Practice

The practical direction emerging from this discourse is clear: before responding immediately to a word one should observe what feeling arose in us; one should come to know what need is behind that feeling; one should try to listen to such a need in the other person’s words as well. Thereafter our request should be stated as clearly and respectfully as possible. Even if we are unable to do this process completely all at once, every conversation becomes a practice opportunity.

Just as through meditation we have learned to observe the movements within us, in relationships too we can learn to observe the difference among feelings, judgments, and needs. Listening, understanding, and expressing the need with respect — this teaching reminds us that these three actions take us in the direction of increasing peace in our words.

01. Let Us Understand the Deeper Purpose of a Festival

Dear friends! Coming together every day during Brahmi Muhurta to meditate, gain knowledge, and encourage ourselves in spiritual practice is immensely valuable. The Jnanodayam programme continues to offer us this opportunity. Now, on the occasion of Devi Navratri and Vijayadashami, let us explore the significance of these festivals and the deeper meaning behind them.

In India, we celebrate festivals with great joy. We dance, decorate our surroundings, and take part in celebrations. But if we celebrate a festival merely as a ritual and return to our old way of life the very next day, we have not received its full benefit. Every festival has a meaning; every festival has a higher purpose. Our ancestors, sages, and yogis passed on many subtle truths they had realised through experience, giving them the form of customs and traditions.

Navratri is especially important for meditators and seekers of Self-knowledge. When we understand the spiritual principle behind an outward celebration, the festival itself becomes part of our practice. Listening to a story simply as a story gives us one level of understanding. But when we discover the story behind the story—the spiritual secret hidden within it—our understanding becomes much deeper.

02. Bathukamma — Honouring Life and the Creative Power of Existence

In the Telangana region, people celebrate the Bathukamma festival during this season. Consider the word Bathukamma. Bathuku means life. We can connect this name with the idea of celebrating the life we have been given. Whenever a word, a custom, or a tradition takes shape, there is a deeper significance behind it. We must discover that significance.

In this context, let us recall the account of Sri Krishna's birth in the Bhagavatam. According to the Puranic narrative, a soul plan had been formed for Sri Krishna to incarnate as Devaki's eighth child and bring about the destruction of Kamsa. The children born before him had died at Kamsa's hands. When the eighth child was born, the infants were exchanged. The baby girl who came into Kamsa's hands is described in the story as Mahamaya, Jaganmata, or Durga Mata.

The Purana describes Kamsa seizing the infant by her feet and attempting to kill her. In the teaching we are discussing, it is said that Mahamaya touched the Earth at that moment, asked Mahavishnu what her role on Earth would be, and was told that Parameshwari would have a very important role to play in the world in the times to come. Rather than seeing this only as an outward event, we should also understand it as a story pointing to the power of the Divine Mother that pervades all creation.

Spiritual tradition explains that creation manifests from the fundamental principle we call Parameshwara and Parameshwari. We should learn to perceive the life force and creativity present throughout nature as expressions of Jaganmata. That is why it is important to explore the Puranic stories associated with our festivals from a meditative and spiritual perspective.

A thought to remember: Look beyond the characters in a story and understand the principles of creation that those characters represent.

03. Immersion — The Principle of Creation, Sustenance, and Dissolution

During festivals, we fashion a form of the deity, decorate it, worship it, and finally immerse it in water. Recently, during the Ganesh festivities, we also performed Ganesh immersion. We should not regard immersion merely as a ritual marking the end of a celebration. Behind it lies the profound principle of creation, sustenance, and dissolution—srishti, sthiti, and laya.

Whatever is created remains in existence for a time and eventually dissolves. Everything that assumes a form must one day release that form. The same principle applies to our physical bodies. We have come into this world, and one day we will leave this physical form behind. Immersion therefore reminds us of this ceaseless cycle in nature.

If we observe the sequence of forming an image, decorating it, worshipping it, and finally immersing it, we can see a pattern resembling creation, sustenance, and dissolution. When we examine the practices established by our ancestors from this perspective, the yogic secrets within them gradually become clear.

04. The Asura Who Devoured Himself — A Symbol of the Dissolution of Ego

Whenever I visit temples, I observe their sculptures, architectural features, and the deeper meanings behind them. Sometimes I also meditate there, trying to understand the energy of the place through direct experience. On one such occasion, I noticed a sculpture of an asura in a Shiva temple.

This asura had a strange nature: he devoured everything he saw. Whatever came before him, he consumed. Eventually, when nothing else remained to eat, he began devouring his own body. First his legs, then his arms, and then the rest of his body—until he had eaten himself up to the neck. Only his head remained. At that point, he became aware that if he consumed even that, his very existence would come to an end.

There is a symbol here that relates to spiritual practice. We have taken form in creation as individual beings. We have names, hometowns, relationships, and countless identifications such as “this is mine” and “this is me.” By gathering these identifications together, we construct a limited definition of “I.” That is the sense of ego.

As we progress along the path of meditation, this limited sense of “I” must gradually dissolve. There is no need to understand the act of devouring oneself in a literal, physical sense. It is a spiritual metaphor for the dissolution of ego, personal limitations, and self-centred identities. The journey towards liberation means moving beyond this small sense of “I” into the vastness of our true Self.

05. The Manipura Chakra and the Hara Centre — Going Beyond Personal Will

Where does the ego operate? From where does the personal will arise that insists, “I,” “mine,” and “I alone must do this”? In this context, we can speak of the Manipura chakra and the Hara centre in the abdominal region. We associate this centre with personal will.

We can also look at the episode of Ravana's defeat in the Ramayana as a symbol. In the narrative, cutting off Ravana's heads does not bring about his death; his defeat finally comes when an arrow strikes the abdominal region. We can examine this story through the spiritual symbolism of ego and the centre of will. This interpretation should be understood as yogic symbolism used in the discourse, not as a medical claim about human anatomy.

Our personal will constantly operates through thoughts such as “I must do this,” “I must achieve that,” and “I want this.” But that will alone is not our true identity. We are not limited to our names, hometowns, or physical bodies. Spiritual knowledge teaches that the Self is boundless and all-pervading. To experience that vastness, we must go beyond the limited idea of “I.”

This is the inner meaning of immersion: as we watch an outwardly created form dissolve, we are reminded that the ego within us, too, must gradually melt away.

06. Why Are the Nine Days Called “Nights”?

A question arises here: why are the celebrations of the Divine Mother called Navratri—the “nine nights”? Should we not practise during the daytime? What is so special about the night?

Ordinarily, we associate night with tamas, sleep, and rest. But for yogis and meditators, the night can be seen differently. When the world is asleep, there is quiet all around. There are fewer interruptions from everyday activities. While others are sleeping, a practitioner has an opportunity to journey inward.

Practices such as waking at three in the morning to meditate and setting aside time for spiritual practice during Brahmi Muhurta gain significance from this perspective. During the day, we have work, family responsibilities, and other worldly activities. We can use the night for spiritual practice. For a seeker, therefore, night is not merely a time for sleep; it can also be a favourable time for the inward journey.

These are symbolic categories used for spiritual practice, not fixed descriptions of women and men. Everyone can practise turning inward.

Notice how much of our energy is scattered outward: through speech, thoughts, activity, and unnecessary attractions. For these nine days, let us change that direction.

Instead of scattering energy outside, let us gather it within ourselves and use it for spiritual practice. There are many mysteries and profound insights within us. We need energy to discover and experience them.

The central purpose of these nine days is to experience the Para Shakti described as lying dormant within us.

Practice principle: Stop wasting energy through needless outward distraction. Redirect attention and energy inward.

04. The Three Shaktis — Meditation, Service, and Self-Knowledge

We worship Para Shakti in many forms: Saraswati Mata, Lakshmi Mata, Durga Mata, and Kali Mata. Though their names and forms differ, we are honouring the same Divine Energy.

Let us remember three essential spiritual practices through these forms:

  • Durga/Kali Shakti — Meditation: Turning inward and experiencing the energy within.
  • Lakshmi Shakti — Service: Using our energy for the wellbeing of others.
  • Saraswati Shakti — Self-knowledge: Understanding our true nature.

Dhyana, Seva, and Atma Jnana — meditation, service, and Self-knowledge — form the practice of the three Shaktis. These three can help us progress when we feel spiritually stuck.

Tradition also speaks of bija aksharas, seed syllables associated with forms of Shakti. Because the automatic captions are unclear about the exact correspondences in this passage, this edition does not supply a definitive mapping.

05. Devi Samarpana — Seeing the Mother in Everyone

Let us spend these nine days in Devi Samarpana, the spirit of surrender to the Divine Mother. Remain connected with yourself in everything you do. Learn to see Jaganmata in everyone.

When you look into a mirror, see the Mother within yourself. When you meet another person, recognise that same Shakti there. Whether the person is a woman or a man, cultivate the feeling that Para Shakti is manifesting through that individual.

This is not merely a matter of calling everyone “Amma.” Our way of seeing must change. Instead of seeing people only through their names, appearances, and social roles, let us learn to see them as expressions of that energy. Let us extend this feeling to trees, animals, nature, and the five great elements.

06. The Symbol of Shiva and Shakti

Shiva symbolises consciousness. Tradition says that without Shakti, even Shiva would be like shava — an inert body. The deeper meaning is that without energy, there is no manifestation. That is why we must first understand Shakti.

In a story associated with the fierce form of the Divine Mother, she destroys the asuras and manifests overwhelming energy. Shiva is said to lie beneath her feet to bring that force to awareness. When her foot touches his heart, awareness arises.

Let us understand this as a symbolic teaching about the relationship between energy and consciousness, rather than only as an outward event.

07. Feeling the Presence of the Divine Mother

Recall the precious moments you spent with your parents. Even when they are not physically before you, the bond you formed allows you to feel their presence in your heart. Their companionship lives in your memories.

In the same way, during these nine days let us feel the presence of Adi Shakti. Just as we have physical parents, we may regard Shakti as our spiritual mother and consciousness as our spiritual father.

The sages and yogis gave many names and forms to the formless Divine Energy. Those forms help us establish a relationship. But we should not stop at the form. At first, we worship the Mother as though she were separate from us. As practice deepens, we move towards the experience that we are not separate from that energy.

Remember: A form can help us establish a connection; the deeper purpose of practice is to experience the presence of Shakti.

08. Lakshmi Mudra — A Symbol of Inwardness

During these nine days, even our sitting posture can serve as a reminder. In this discourse, placing the left leg over the right and the left hand over the right is explained as a symbolic expression of Lakshmi Mudra.

The intention is to give priority to the Shakti principle and remember not to scatter our energy outward.

We may have many spiritual aspirations: to understand the Ajna chakra, experience Sahasrara, attain Self-knowledge, or release the burden of karma. But wishing alone is not enough. We must practise. Otherwise, as the proverb suggests, we remain exactly where we were. Spiritual progress requires us to use our energy purposefully.

09. Total Acceptance

Another important practice for these nine days is Total Acceptance.

Some things in life please us; others do not. We may like sweetness and dislike the bitterness of bitter gourd. People, too, have different temperaments. Some speak sweetly; others may feel difficult to us.

Let us learn to accept people as they are. Reduce the expectations that someone must change in a particular way to suit us. Accept yourself as you are, too.

Only when we recognise and accept our present state can we move to another state. Acceptance does not mean abandoning growth. It means ceasing to reject the reality from which growth must begin.

Write down the circumstances you struggle to accept. Which people do you continually expect to change? What do you reject about yourself?

Practise acceptance from morning until night. As that practice becomes natural, life itself can become a continuous celebration.

10. Dasara — Living Joyfully

Playing with the sound of the word Dasara, the lecture reminds us to live with joy and lightness. The important point is not a linguistic derivation but the invitation to celebrate every moment.

Celebration does not require external objects. Do not assume happiness can come only when a desire is satisfied. We are more than the physical body. Let us discover the joy arising from the Self.

11. Red and White — Symbols in Devi Worship

Red is prominent in worship of the Divine Mother: in kumkum, clothing, and decorations. In this discourse, red represents dynamic, manifest energy. White and red are also used as symbolic colours.

Do not see these merely as decorations. Our sages embedded teachings in stories, festivals, and customs. A festival has two purposes: to celebrate and to learn and practise spiritual wisdom.

12. Chandra Nadi, Surya Nadi, and Sushumna

Breathing through the two nostrils does not always feel identical; airflow may seem stronger on one side at a given moment. Yogic tradition associates the left nostril with Chandra Nadi and the right with Surya Nadi.

Here, Chandra Nadi is associated with Shakti and inwardness, while Surya Nadi is associated with outward activity. The feeling of balanced breathing during meditation is explained symbolically through Sushumna Nadi and the upward movement of energy. These are explanations within yogic tradition, not established anatomical descriptions.

Let us now connect these ideas with the practice of Devi Samarpana.

13. Bowing the Head — Surrendering the Ego

In our culture, we bow before elders, parents, and deities. In some temples, entrances become smaller as we approach the inner shrine, prompting us to lower our heads. We can understand this as a symbol.

We use expressions such as “it has gone to his head” when someone becomes proud of wealth, status, or power. To bow the head can symbolise letting go of ego.

We honour our parents, through whom we entered physical life. In the same spirit, let us bow with surrender before Jaganmata, the Shakti understood as the source of creation.

14. The Devi Samarpana Breath-and-Bowing Practice

Let us learn the practice introduced today. Its purpose is not to bend the body mechanically. The essential feeling is: “I offer my ego, my problems, and my aspirations at the feet of the Divine Mother.”

Sequence demonstrated in the discourse

  1. Sit comfortably. If using a chair, choose a position appropriate for your body. You may remember the symbolic intention of Lakshmi Mudra.
  2. The discourse demonstrates Vishnu Mudra with the right hand and gently closing the right nostril.
  3. It demonstrates taking the breath in through the left nostril in nine small successive stages. This is a specific pranayama technique, not ordinary relaxed breathing.
  4. The hands are then brought into a gesture of salutation. While breathing out, the head is lowered slowly with the feeling of Devi Samarpana.
  5. In the bowed position, the practitioner is invited to count to nine or inwardly repeat the bija syllable “Shreem”, without chanting it aloud.
  6. Return upright slowly. The discourse demonstrates nine cycles and recommends the practice in the morning and, when possible, in the evening.

Important safety note: The demonstration also includes kumbhaka (breath retention), bahya kumbhaka (holding after exhalation), and bending the head. These are not appropriate for everyone. People with neck or spinal problems, dizziness, respiratory or cardiac conditions should not attempt breath-retention versions without suitable professional guidance. The feeling of surrender can also be practised while sitting comfortably and breathing naturally.

The intention during the nine cycles

Throughout the demonstration, the central reminder is to bow with the inner feeling of “Sharana, Sharana” — I surrender. Offer the problems you cannot resolve, the obstacles you find difficult to cross, and your aspirations for spiritual growth to the Divine Mother.

The final cycle is performed independently, without spoken guidance, in full awareness. The intention of surrender matters more than the number alone.

15. Remembering the Mother's Presence Throughout the Day

After morning meditation, begin the day in the spirit of surrender. Continue to remember the Mother's presence in everyone you meet.

The invitation for these nine days is to deepen our connection with the Mother principle. This does not diminish Shiva or consciousness; the focus of this particular practice is first to establish a connection with Shakti.

The discourse expresses the hope that sincere practice may bring us to a new spiritual state by Vijayadashami. Rather than measuring progress by extraordinary external events, we can observe changes in inwardness, acceptance, surrender, and joy.

16. Daily Practice — The Essential Reminders

  • Devi Samarpana: Offer the ego to the Shakti within.
  • Presence: Remember Jaganmata within yourself and others.
  • Lakshmi Mudra as a symbol: Turn from outward distraction towards inward awareness.
  • Total Acceptance: Accept people, situations, and your present state.
  • Celebration: Live joyfully without waiting for every desire to be fulfilled.
  • Meditation, Service, and Self-knowledge: Bring the three Shaktis into daily practice.

Topic: Devi Navratri Wisdom — From the Deva–Asura Principle to Intention and Surrender

01. Suras and Asuras — When Rhythm Becomes Discord

We speak of swara and apaswara in speech and music. If we say something inappropriate to the occasion, it creates discord. If a musical note falls out of rhythm, we call it apaswara. Similarly, life has its own rhythm, order, and connection.

When our body, mind, heart, and inner being are in harmony, we live with greater balance. When that connection is lost, discord arises within. Let us understand the Puranic imagery of suras and asuras through this comparison.

This is a spiritual interpretation, not a claim about the historical etymology of the words sura and asura.

02. Breath as Swara — Observing the Rhythm of Life

Breath is the closest spiritual instrument we have. It supports speech and the body's essential activities. Yogic teachings give it particular significance when speaking about prana shakti.

Observing breath brings us towards the present moment. It helps us notice the relationship between our body, mind, and feelings. By attending to its natural rhythm, we can become more aware of our inner state.

Before trying to change the breath, observe it. Meditation helps us recognise the discord within and cultivate greater harmony.

03. Birth Does Not Determine Spiritual Greatness

Consider Ravana. He practised austerities, mastered music, and acquired knowledge. Yet arrogance, possessiveness, and harmful tendencies remained within him. Spiritual practice or learning alone does not guarantee divine conduct.

We cannot determine a person's spiritual stature by birth. The real measure is Self-knowledge and the recognition of the same Self in all beings. When we experience the truth expressed as Sarvam Khalvidam Brahma, our actions naturally turn towards the wellbeing of the world.

Continuous practice, service, and wishing for the happiness of all beings are qualities worth cultivating.

04. The Three Gunas — Tamas, Rajas, and Sattva

When tamas predominates, we may become lethargic, avoid necessary work, or act from anger, violence, or pride. Even our good actions may become entangled in a constant desire for praise.

When rajas becomes excessive, we may chase recognition, fame, and prestige without rest. Working and achieving are not wrong. The problem begins when the craving for recognition takes away our peace. Anger may subside for a while, only to return in the same pattern.

Cultivating sattva means developing clarity, balance, service, and inward awareness. The first step is to observe the qualities operating within us.

Central insight: Before looking for asuras outside, observe how ego, possessiveness, aggression, anger, and the desire for praise operate within yourself.

The King Who Had to Remove His Inner Crown

Consider the story of a king who visited a teacher seeking Self-knowledge. He arrived wearing a heavy golden crown, armour, and jewels. When the teacher told him to remove the burdens he carried, the king removed these ornaments. But the teacher meant something deeper: remove the crown in your mind—the conviction that “I am the king; I am greater than others.” Taking off outward symbols does not remove inner pride.

Similarly, a person may resolve to avoid anger for a few days or even forty days. If the inner pattern remains unchanged, anger can return. Spiritual change requires releasing attachment within, not only observing an external rule for a short time.

05. The Deva–Asura Battle Is Within Us

The Puranas often describe battles between devas and asuras. Do not read these stories only as external events. They also point to a struggle between tendencies within ourselves.

Even while meditating, we may experience jealousy, anger, attachment, and pride. One day we are peaceful; the next day, a small incident makes us angry. This is the inner struggle.

Meditation is a movement towards our divine nature. Which qualities are growing in us? Which are carrying us away from our deeper Self? Keep asking these questions.

06. Ravana's Story — Practice Does Not Excuse Crossing Boundaries

Ravana performed great austerities. Yet he violated Sita Mata's freedom by taking her away without her consent. Failing to respect another person's autonomy and consent is an example of asuric conduct.

No matter how much knowledge, power, or skill we acquire, how we use it matters. Spiritual practice should make us more humble and responsible.

07. The Three Stages of Navratri

During Navratri, we remember three principal forms of Shakti.

The first three days — Mahadurga: Awaken the will to overcome obstacles, limiting patterns, and difficulties that repeatedly hold us back.

The next three days — Mahalakshmi: Cultivate divine qualities, confidence, inner resources, and a sense of abundance.

The final three days — Mahasaraswati: Move towards discernment, clarity, and Self-knowledge.

These stages belong to one spiritual journey: recognise and overcome obstacles; nurture noble qualities; become established in wisdom.

08. Why Do We Turn to the Mother?

As children, when we needed something, we often went first to our mother. When we were hurt or troubled, we told her. I remember wanting a toy as a child. It felt difficult to ask my father directly, so I would approach my mother. Somehow, she would speak to him and help.

Let us bring that feeling of closeness to Jaganmata. Offer our difficulties, obstacles, and limitations to that Shakti. We approach Mahadurga as the power that helps us cross what seems difficult.

Here, the Mother is not merely an external deity. We are also remembering the Adi Shakti within ourselves.

To Be Close to the Self Is to Live in Harmony

We can describe the divine tendency in another way: the closer we come to our own Self, the more we live in harmony. When we identify ourselves only with the physical body and pursue every outward attraction, we move away from that inner connection. Beauty, wealth, and possessions change. Building our identity entirely around them gives rise to pride, attachment, attraction, and aversion. Meditation helps us return to the Self.

Fasting from Mental Noise and Social Media

During these nine days, fasting need not refer only to food. The discourse emphasises mind fasting—reducing unnecessary thought and cultivating inner silence—and social-media fasting—taking a break from reels, constant phone use, and distracting entertainment. Let us reclaim time and energy for practice. The lecture also mentions reducing meals, but dietary restriction should be adapted to individual health needs. Children, pregnant people, and anyone with diabetes or other medical conditions should not undertake restrictive fasting without appropriate medical advice.

09. Ichha Shakti — Give Energy a Direction

Desire itself is not wrong. Without intention, how would we act? But we need to discover what we truly seek beneath our desires.

Which obstacles keep returning? What limitations prevent us from progressing in practice? Recognise them clearly.

Energy needs direction. Write down your goals. Even if your ultimate life purpose is not yet clear, identify the direction in which you want to grow now. Do not let your energy scatter without intention.

Work for the welfare of the world also needs intention and action, whether through teaching meditation, promoting vegetarianism, or serving through a project.

10. What Do I Really Want?

Ask yourself, “What do I want?” The first answer may not be the deepest one. Behind one desire lies another; behind that lies a feeling we wish to experience.

For example: “I want this family conflict resolved.” Why? “I want peace of mind.” Why? “Then I can meditate more.” Why meditate more? “So I can remain connected with myself.” What does that bring? Inner joy and fulfilment.

Keep asking until the feeling beneath the outward wish becomes clear.

Reflection: Ask, “If this happens, what will I gain?” Repeating this question can reveal the deeper experience you are truly seeking.

11. A Participant's Example — From Family Stability to Inner Fulfilment

A participant shared her wish that her husband obtain a good job and a higher position. He had the qualifications, she explained, and she felt that if the family became more secure, she could progress spiritually.

Let us look more closely. What would a better job for her husband actually give her? Stability, fewer worries, time for practice, and ultimately a feeling of fulfilment.

The outward wish may be for employment; the deeper longing may be for peace, contentment, and the joy of the Self. Recognising this can help us approach our intentions with greater awareness.

12. Gurus, Intentions, and Spiritual Desires

Some desires concern worldly life. Others concern spiritual growth: enlightenment, liberation, opening the third eye, or experiencing higher spiritual states.

A traditional spiritual story involving Lahiri Mahasaya and Mahavatar Babaji was mentioned to illustrate the relationship between a guru, a disciple's desire, and moving beyond desire. This is presented as a traditional spiritual narrative, not as an independently verified historical event.

Whatever the scale of our intention, remember to ask: “What is it that I truly want?”

13. Surrender to Mahadurga — Nine Bowing Cycles

Let us revisit the Devi Samarpana practice introduced on Day 2. The demonstration again includes sitting posture, breathing through a selected nostril, namaskara mudra, bowing the head, and inwardly remembering the seed syllable Shreem.

The discourse recommends nine cycles. In each cycle, the most important element is not the bodily movement but the feeling of offering the ego to Jaganmata. Imagine placing your desires, obstacles, and intentions at the feet of Mahadurga.

After the practice, remain still and silent, allowing the feeling of surrender to settle.

Safety note: The source demonstration includes closing a nostril, staged inhalation, breath retention, and bending the head. These techniques may not be safe for everyone. People with neck or spine conditions, heart or respiratory problems, or dizziness should not attempt them without appropriate in-person professional guidance. You can cultivate the feeling of surrender while sitting comfortably and breathing naturally.

14. Surrender Must Be Accompanied by Action

Offering our intentions to the Divine Mother does not mean abandoning our responsibilities. We must still do what is ours to do. Take the practical steps that correspond to your intention, even if those steps are small.

Move forward with the trust that the rest is offered to Shakti. This attitude can help us act without being overwhelmed by fear.

Surrender is not passivity. Practice, intention, action, and acceptance must work together.

15. Questions — Family Property, Unity, and Service

A participant described a family property matter. She wished for unity among five sisters, a resolution of the matter, and the opportunity to use the outcome for the welfare of others. She also expressed intentions for financial abundance, peace, and liberation.

Let us remember that it is not wrong to seek solutions to external matters. But peace, unity, and service must grow within us too. Spiritual intention should accompany responsible family communication and necessary practical steps.

16. Childhood Illness — “Training, Not Punishment”

Nagaveni raised an important question. We speak of karma as training rather than punishment. But what about young children with serious or chronic illnesses, who do not yet have the understanding to interpret their suffering spiritually? How can it be called training?

In response, the discourse emphasised that children must not be forced to meditate. When parents practise themselves, children may naturally imitate them. Give children freedom and lead by example.

The speaker also presented a spiritual belief that souls plan lessons before birth. This is a spiritual worldview expressed in the lecture, not a medical explanation of childhood illness.

Important clarification: A child's illness must not be treated as proof of wrongdoing, deserved punishment, or a burden the child chose. Adults remain responsible for providing appropriate medical care, love, protection, and support.

The Role of Adults in Caring for Children

In answering Nagaveni's question, the discourse explains that children learn under the care of parents and teachers. When a child experiences illness, adults must provide the care that the child needs. The speaker uses the example of an autistic child to emphasise unconditional love, patience, and understanding. Children may voluntarily imitate adults who meditate, but they should not be forced to meditate.

The discussion also includes spiritual interpretations involving karma, reincarnation, past-life relationships, and situations supposedly co-created by family members. These are matters of spiritual belief, not established causes of childhood illness, and must not be used to blame children or parents. Medical care, developmental support, and family support remain essential.

17. The Power of Gurus and Respect for Individual Freedom

The later discussion referred to revered teachers, including Neem Karoli Baba, and accounts of extraordinary events associated with them. It also emphasised respecting each soul's journey and freedom.

Such accounts belong to spiritual tradition. They do not replace medical treatment or scientific assessment.

18. A Devotional Song to the Mother

Following the questions, a devotional song to the Divine Mother was performed. The captions include phrases such as Omkararupini, Kleemkara Vasini, and Prakriti Swarupini. The gathering concluded by remembering the presence of Jaganmata through music.

The Central Teaching of Day 3

Suras and asuras are within us. Observe the rhythm of breath. Recognise pride, attachment, anger, and the craving for recognition; move towards service, balance, and Self-knowledge. Invoke Mahadurga to face obstacles, Mahalakshmi to nurture divine qualities, and Mahasaraswati to cultivate wisdom. Discover the deeper fulfilment behind your desires, and combine surrender with responsible action.

Cause, Symbolism, and Spiritual Rebirth

On the fourth day of Devi Navratri, Dr. Newton explores the story of Rambha and Karambha as a way to understand two impulses within us: the desire to engage with worldly life and the longing for liberation. Through stories about the mind, impressions, and inner freedom, he invites listeners to approach the festival as a living spiritual practice rather than merely a celebration.

Celebrating Navratri Through Understanding

Good morning, my dear friends! As we celebrate Devi Navratri, let us not stop at remembering the names and forms of Jagannmata, Adi Shakti, and Para Shakti. Let us try to understand and experience the spiritual insight represented by each day.

Every event has a cause. Our birth has a cause; the stories of the devas and asuras also have contexts and purposes. Unless we examine the background of a story, its deeper teaching can escape us. The sages conveyed practical insights through festivals and symbolic narratives. A festival becomes a spiritual celebration when its teachings begin to change how we live.

Just as a child spends nine months in the mother's womb before being born, these nine nights can symbolize a new inner birth: a growing recognition of the divine energy within us. Let us approach the origin of Mahishasura through that lens.

Rambha and Karambha — Two Longings Within Us

The story speaks of two brothers, Rambha and Karambha. In this lecture, their austerities are interpreted as expressions of two inner tendencies.

Rambha represents worldly desire. We want possessions, success, recognition, and circumstances to unfold according to our expectations. When life does not obey our wishes, disappointment, anger, or sorrow can arise. Our attention remains entangled in external events.

Rambha's austerity in water is read symbolically: water suggests Maya and the depths of emotion. Just as the surface of the sea conceals what lies beneath, the visible world can seem to be the whole of reality. We may blame others for our suffering without examining the habitual responses within us.

Karambha represents the determination to practise without being lost in Maya. His austerity amid the five fires and beneath a banyan tree is related to the challenge posed by the five senses. Sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch continually draw our attention outward. The task is to discover inward steadiness amid those attractions.

Do not regard these brothers only as people in an ancient story. Both tendencies live in us: the wish to fulfil our responsibilities and desires, and the longing to know our true nature. We need not abandon our work or families; neither should we forget the deeper purpose of our lives.

Indra and the Crocodile — When the Mind Interrupts Practice

In the narrative, Indra appears in the form of a crocodile and pulls the ascetic into the water. Dr. Newton interprets Indra, traditionally associated with the senses, as a symbol of the mind.

Observe what happens when you sit to meditate. Memories arise. Someone's words return. Worries about tomorrow appear. Even when you intend to rest your attention on the breath, the mind repeatedly draws you outward. The crocodile becomes an image of this pull.

The real struggle is not with other people but with our own unexamined reactions, habits, and restless thoughts. Another episode speaks of offering the head into fire. This is explained as the symbolic surrender of the mind and ego, not as an act of physical harm to imitate.

Clarity of Purpose — Samsara and Nirvana

My dear friends, why have we come into this life? What is it that we truly wish to accomplish? Education, livelihood, family, and earning a living all matter. But the activities necessary for survival should not automatically become the whole purpose of our existence.

Adults sometimes decide what their children must become, projecting their own unrealized ambitions onto them. Inner observation helps us recognize which aspirations are truly ours.

Rambha and Karambha illuminate the tension between Samsara, with its practical duties and pleasures, and Nirvana, the longing for freedom from suffering and for Self-knowledge. Rejecting family life does not by itself create spiritual maturity. Nor does endless pursuit of external success bring lasting freedom. The teaching is to live responsibly while cultivating inward independence.

Putra and Dvija — The Meaning of a New Birth

The image of surrendering the head to fire points toward relinquishing ego and turning toward the Self. In the lecture, the word putra is given a symbolic interpretation linked to freedom from the cycle of birth and death. Dvija, or 'twice-born', is explored as a second birth through knowledge.

These are spiritual interpretations offered in the lecture, not claims that they are the sole or standard linguistic meanings of these terms.

A calendar date changing does not make our life new if our old habits remain intact. A spiritual new birth begins when the way we see ourselves and respond to life begins to change.

The Halwa Example — Suppression Is Not Freedom

Imagine someone who loves halwa and announces, 'I have renounced it; I will never eat it again.' Yet as soon as the person sits for meditation, thoughts of halwa return. The desire rejected outwardly remains active inwardly.

Suppressing a desire is different from becoming free of it. Forceful rejection may leave the attraction untouched. Instead, the practitioner must observe desire and understand how the mind sustains it.

The lecture also evokes the image of a drop of water resting on a lotus leaf without clinging to it. In the same way, we can participate in family, work, and society without losing our inner freedom through attachment.

Ramana Maharshi's Story — Who Steals Our Peace?

Dr. Newton recalls a story about thieves entering Ramanasramam. While some disciples reacted angrily, Ramana Maharshi remained calm. The incident is used to point toward the mind as the thief that can steal our happiness.

Someone says something hurtful. The event itself may last a moment, but the mind repeats it for hours: 'Why did they speak that way? Why did they insult me?' The inner story grows larger than the original event.

This does not mean tolerating injustice or refusing necessary action. We can respond appropriately while observing how anger, resentment, and the wish for retaliation arise within us.

A question for practice: Is my peace being disturbed by the event itself, or by the story my mind keeps telling about it?

Vasanas and Samskaras — The Soiled Cloth

Consider a dirty cloth being washed. The visible dirt may disappear after the first wash, yet an unpleasant smell remains. Further washing is needed before the cloth is truly clean.

The story illustrates vasanas and samskaras, the tendencies and impressions that continue to influence our responses. We may believe anger has disappeared, only to find it returning under pressure. We may think pride has dissolved, yet criticism still hurts.

A single meditation session does not necessarily erase longstanding tendencies. Continuous awareness, practice, and inner purification are needed. We learn to recognize the influence of tamasic and rajasic habits rather than assume they have vanished.

The Gold Mine in Our Own Backyard

A poor man is told by a wise person that a treasure lies buried in his own backyard. He does not believe it and lives without discovering it. Someone else eventually takes the advice, digs, and finds the treasure.

The hidden gold represents the wisdom and spiritual potential already within us. We may search everywhere outside while neglecting the inward exploration urged by the teachers. Hearing about the treasure is not enough: we must undertake the practice that reveals it.

The Mosquito and the Elephant — A Wider Sense of Self

A mosquito rests on an elephant and later apologizes for any inconvenience it may have caused. The elephant replies that it had not even noticed the mosquito's presence.

The mosquito represents a narrow self-preoccupation; the elephant suggests the vastness and steadiness of the Self. Small incidents can seem enormous when we identify completely with our personal pride. A wider understanding of who we are can help us meet them without being shaken by everything that happens.

Devi Samarpana — The Spirit of Surrender

The lecture closes by returning to Devi Samarpana. Dr. Newton demonstrates a practice involving a Lakshmi-mudra orientation, attention to the left side, breathing through the left nostril while gently closing the right, bowing in a gesture of reverence, and inward remembrance of the bija syllable Shreem (శ్రీం). Nine rounds are mentioned.

The central meaning is not forceful control of breathing, but the surrender of ego. Remembering Jagannmata, Adi Shakti, and Para Shakti becomes an invitation to quieten the mind and cultivate humility.

Bringing the Teaching into Daily Life

Worldly responsibilities and the longing for liberation need not be enemies. We can meet our duties without becoming imprisoned by them. We can understand desires rather than merely suppress them, notice when the mind magnifies suffering, and steadily examine the vasanas and samskaras that shape our reactions. The gold mine of inner potential is not elsewhere. The invitation of this fourth day is to discover it through awareness, practice, and the surrender of ego.

Main Teachings

The Two Navratris — Two Complementary Realizations

Friends, Navratri is not merely a festival. It is an opportunity to deepen our practice. In this lecture, Dr. Newton relates the Navratri observed in Chaitra to Aham Brahmasmi—the recognition of divinity within oneself—and the Navratri observed in Ashwayuja to Sarvam Khalvidam Brahma—the recognition of divinity in all creation.

The first insight invites us to stop searching for the Divine as something entirely separate from ourselves. Yet even spiritual understanding can give rise to a subtle thought: I know, while others do not. The second insight challenges that separation. The same sacred presence we seek within is to be recognized in other people and in the natural world. The lecture links this outlook to compassion and responsibility toward trees, rivers, mountains, animals, and fellow human beings. Spiritual knowledge should change how we live, not remain only a remembered phrase.

Rambha and Karambha — Desire, Awareness, and Practice

The story of Rambha and Karambha is read as a reflection of the two currents within a human life. One current moves toward livelihood, family responsibilities, possessions, and worldly desires. The other asks whether there is a deeper truth beyond all that we accumulate. Dr. Newton cautions against believing that forceful suppression of desire is the same as understanding it. A desire pushed out of sight may reappear in another form.

The practice is to observe desires with awareness, to recognize their movement without becoming their servant. In this symbolic reading, Karambha's austerity points toward the earnest longing for liberation and the determination to understand what lies beyond repeated patterns of attachment.

Reading Mythological Stories for Their Inner Meaning

The lecture invites listeners to consider symbolic meanings in stories about offspring, ritual, and the buffalo. These are not presented merely as accounts of outward events. Dr. Newton connects them to questions of bondage, liberation, and identification with nature. Such readings are the speaker's spiritual interpretations; they should not be mistaken for the sole historical or linguistic meaning of the traditional stories.

Why Six Emissaries? The Six Inner Adversaries

Mahishasura sends six emissaries to the Divine Mother. Why six? Dr. Newton interprets them as kama, krodha, lobha, moha, mada, and matsarya—desire, anger, greed, attachment, pride, and jealousy. These enemies are not merely characters outside us. They can be found in our habits, reactions, judgments, and attachments.

The aim of meditation is not to wage war on other people. It is to recognize the patterns that limit our freedom. Recognition comes first, awareness follows, and transformation becomes possible. In this sense, the Mother's victory represents the overcoming of inner bondage.

The First Emissary — Desire and Its Restlessness

The first emissary represents kama, the tendency to be governed by desire. The lecture describes someone who already possesses a comfortable home but becomes consumed by the wish for a grander residence after seeing a palace. In chasing what is missing, he loses the ability to appreciate what is present.

There is a difference between pursuing a reasonable wish and surrendering one's peace to it. A desire fulfilled can soon be replaced by another. Awareness helps us see this cycle before it becomes the center of our lives.

Durmukha — The Destructive Force of Anger

Durmukha represents krodha, anger. Dr. Newton asks us to notice even the change in our facial expression when anger takes hold. The teaching includes the example of a sculptor who has worked carefully on an image but, enraged by a small imperfection, damages his own work. A moment of fury can undo the results of prolonged effort.

Anger may appear to be directed at someone else, yet it first disturbs the person experiencing it. The lesson is to become aware of that disturbance before it governs our speech or actions.

Tamramukha / Tamra — Greed and the Fear of Losing

The emissary associated with lobha, greed, brings attention to the anxiety of guarding wealth. A wealthy person becomes so preoccupied with protecting possessions that he cannot eat or sleep peacefully. In the story, thieves eventually take what he feared losing.

The lecture contrasts hoarding with sharing. Its central spiritual point is not a literal financial formula but an invitation to generosity: knowledge, help, and resources become meaningful when used for the good of others. A true teacher does not conceal wisdom merely to preserve personal importance.

Chichura — When Attachment Disguises Itself as Love

The fourth emissary is linked to moha, deluded attachment. The examples of Dhritarashtra, Gandhari, and Duryodhana raise an important distinction between loving one's children and defending every action they take. Care sometimes requires guidance, correction, and the willingness to acknowledge mistakes.

Alongside education and livelihood, Dr. Newton emphasizes giving children a foundation of meditation, self-knowledge, and values. Attachment becomes harmful when it prevents us from seeing clearly.

Asiloma — The Pride of 'I Know Everything'

Asiloma represents mada, pride. Even a person who has learned something valuable may begin to think there is nothing left to learn. The example of Duryodhana illustrates how arrogance can prevent someone from receiving good counsel.

Spiritual experiences do not make a person superior to others. Real learning should deepen humility. What we know is small compared with what remains to be understood. The ego can survive in a particularly subtle form when it begins to identify itself as spiritually advanced.

Bidala — Jealousy and Comparison

Bidala represents matsarya, jealousy. The lecture draws attention to the suffering created when we compare our appearance, abilities, possessions, or achievements with those of others. A story about a person seeking boons illustrates how envy can become more powerful than gratitude for one's own blessings.

Another person's success need not be understood as our failure. The ability to rejoice in another's well-being gradually weakens the habit of comparison.

Mahishasura — The Subtle Ego That Remains

Even when the six obvious tendencies begin to diminish, another attachment may remain: I am the one who has practiced; I am the one who has attained knowledge. Dr. Newton interprets Mahishasura's final defeat as a symbol of going beyond this spiritual self-importance.

Sarvam Khalvidam Brahma is therefore more than a statement to recite. It is an invitation to cultivate a way of seeing that includes nature, strangers, and even people with whom we disagree. Such a vision asks for humility rather than superiority.

Devi Samarpana — Placing the Ego at the Mother's Feet

The lecture concludes by returning to Devi Samarpana, surrender to the Divine Mother. Dr. Newton explains the inner attitude of bowing, the devotional significance of the gesture, and the remembrance of Shreem (శ్రీం). The essence of the practice is to offer ego, possessiveness, and the six inner tendencies to the Mother with sincerity.

The source also describes specific breathing and bodily movements. Those details are not reproduced here as independent physical instructions, particularly where the automatic transcript does not establish precise timing or technique. What remains central is the teaching's inner movement: from self-centeredness toward awareness, humility, and surrender.

Mahishasura — A Symbol Along the Meditative Path

Friends, the story of Mahishasura need not be approached only as a battle between a goddess and an asura. Dr. Newton invites listeners to see in it the challenges of the inner journey. The story of Mahishasura's austerity on Mount Meru is connected with the search for higher states of awareness.

People report different experiences in meditation. Some describe sensations, images, or a sense of activity associated with the third eye or Sahasrara; others do not. The teaching cautions against turning any one experience into a compulsory milestone. The lecture uses the traditional language of chakras, Sushumna, and the awakening of energy as part of its yogic interpretation, not as a claim that these are established anatomical mechanisms.

The Divine Energy and Our Inner Adversaries

The lecture relates survival-related fears to Muladhara, emotional patterns to Svadhishthana, and aggressive or dominating tendencies to Manipura within its spiritual framework. The important question is not whether we can name a chakra but whether we can recognize fear, attachment, anger, and the impulse to control.

A frightened animal may react defensively. Human beings, too, can respond to perceived threats with anger or aggression. Dr. Newton uses such observations to bring attention to patterns that meditation can help us notice. The battle with Mahishasura becomes a way of speaking about this inward work.

The Eighteen Arms — Many Powers, One Inner Transformation

The image of the Divine Mother with eighteen arms and many weapons can be read as a representation of the powers needed to overcome ignorance and inner bondage. In the story, the energies of the deities come together in the form of the Goddess. Dr. Newton relates this image to the awakening of awareness, strength, and clarity within the practitioner.

The weapons are not an instruction to fight other people. They point toward the transformation of fear, anger, delusion, and pride. The preserved source does not reliably establish a complete, individually verified meaning for every weapon in every hand; therefore no invented eighteen-item list is supplied here.

Waking, Dreaming, Deep Sleep, and Turiya

The lecture turns to jagrat (waking), svapna (dreaming), sushupti (deep sleep), and turiya, the fourth state described in contemplative traditions. During waking, the mind is occupied with the world of the senses. Dreaming brings another field of images and experiences. Deep sleep is distinguished from both. Turiya is discussed as awareness beyond identification with these changing conditions.

Meditative images, colors, or visionary experiences can feel meaningful to the person who has them. Yet the teaching directs attention beyond collecting experiences. The central question is whether the practitioner is becoming more aware, less bound by reactions, and more deeply established in stillness.

Four Ways of Approaching the Divine

Dr. Newton discusses salokya, samipya, sarupya, and sayujya as stages or modes of relationship with the Divine.

Salokya is explained through the sense of being in the same realm or world as the Divine. Samipya conveys nearness. Sarupya points to likeness or identification with the Divine form. Sayujya expresses union, the movement beyond the felt separation between devotee and the Divine Mother.

These terms are presented in the spiritual sense developed in the lecture. The emphasis is not on acquiring a new status or making a claim of superiority, but on allowing the feeling of separateness to soften.

Bringing the Teaching Into the Body and Attitude

The lecture links inward intention with posture and devotional gesture. The body can express reverence through bowing, and the mind can accompany that movement with sincere attention. A gesture made mechanically is different from one in which a person is consciously offering pride and attachment.

In this sense, spiritual practice is not merely a set of movements. Its meaning depends on awareness, humility, and the quality of surrender behind the action.

Devi Samarpana — Surrender Without Separation

The practice of Devi Samarpana is presented as an offering of the limited sense of 'I' to the Divine Mother. Rather than holding tightly to the identity of the one who performs, controls, or achieves, the practitioner is invited to bow with openness.

The deeper movement is from seeing the Mother as entirely outside oneself toward recognizing an inseparable relationship with that presence. This is the devotional and nondual direction in which the lecture develops its teaching.

The Trident — Going Beyond the Three Gunas

The trident, Trishula, is interpreted as a symbol of the three gunas: tamas, rajas, and sattva. The aim of practice is to recognize their influence without remaining bound by them. Dr. Newton relates this to trigunatita, the state beyond identification with the three gunas.

The three points are also associated in the lecture's symbolic explanation with waking, dreaming, and deep sleep. The teaching then turns toward Turiya, the awareness not limited to any one of those states. The trident is therefore not merely a weapon in an external story; it serves as a reminder of the inner journey beyond habitual conditioning.

The sixth day's teaching closes on the movement from separation to surrender and from surrender toward union: not simply to admire the Divine Mother as an image, but to allow the qualities she represents to transform one's life.

The Final Three Days: Awakening the Power of Wisdom

Dr. Newton Kondaveti connects the final three days of Navratri with the awakening of jnana shakti, the power of wisdom. He describes spiritual practice through three complementary powers: iccha shakti (will), kriya shakti (action), and jnana shakti (wisdom), recalling the forms of Mahadurga, Mahalakshmi, and Mahasaraswati. The inner purification cultivated over the first six days prepares the seeker to recognize deeper obstacles with the sword of discernment.

He encourages listeners not to read the battles in the Devi Mahatmyam merely as episodes from mythology. The asuras also represent tendencies operating within the human mind. A story may capture our imagination, but its teaching becomes transformative only when we recognize its relevance to our own lives and put that understanding into practice.

Shumbha and Nishumbha: The Bondage of Pleasure and Pain

The speaker interprets Shumbha as a symbol of attachment to pleasure and Nishumbha as a symbol of attachment to suffering. The urge to secure pleasant experiences and the struggle to avoid painful ones can keep the mind turning in the same circle. He relates vritti, a mental tendency, to the image of a recurring circle, drawing attention to the repetitive character of such patterns.

When pleasure arrives, we want it to remain. Fear of its disappearance can itself become suffering. In suffering, we begin searching for pleasure again. The seeker is therefore invited to examine the ego and possessiveness that sustain this cycle, rather than assuming that the answer lies in obtaining ever more favorable experiences.

Chanda, Munda, Dhumralochana, and Raktabija

The other asuras in Shumbha's retinue are likewise presented as inner tendencies. Chanda points toward self-deception. Munda represents the self-defeating impulse that can undo hard-won progress at the last moment. Dr. Newton illustrates this with the image of someone climbing ninety-nine steps only to slip at the final one.

The name Dhumralochana evokes smoke. Just as smoke obscures our sight, the veil of illusion can cause the mind to mistake what is present for what is absent, and what is absent for what is present. Raktabija represents an even subtler multiplication of ego. In the myth, every drop of his blood gives rise to another Raktabija; inwardly, one small egoic impulse may proliferate into many forms.

These adversaries need not be sought somewhere outside us. Their traces can be observed in our thoughts, reactions, and habits. Recognizing them is the first movement toward freedom from their influence.

There Is No Surrender Without Trust

To support this inner transformation, the speaker emphasizes gathering and directing one's energy through meditation. Trust in the power within, a felt connection to that power, and faith in Devi Shakti form the ground of surrender. Without genuine trust, surrender may remain only a spoken ideal.

It is not enough simply to say that one meditates. The more revealing question is whether meditation has changed one's anger, pride, and self-defeating habits. If spirituality becomes a label or a mask, its deeper purpose is missed. Even family members, Dr. Newton observes, are more likely to trust visible changes in conduct than claims about spiritual attainment.

The Sculptor: Ego Hidden Behind Skill

Dr. Newton tells of a gifted sculptor who fashioned beautiful golden figures and grew proud of his skill, believing that no one could equal him. When a sage visited, the sculptor proudly displayed his work. The sage smiled and pointed to a small clay oil lamp in the corner. However beautiful the golden figures might be, they could break if dropped and could be stolen. The humble clay lamp, by contrast, held a flame that dispelled darkness, without pride or fear. The sage compared the sculptor’s fragile ego to the golden figures and urged him to kindle the lamp of Self-knowledge.

The speaker uses the story to expose subtle forms of ego. Pride may arise from wealth, position, physical beauty, academic degrees, intellectual ability, professional excellence, or even spiritual practice. The lesson is not to reject accomplishment, but to examine whether it has quietly strengthened the thought, “I alone am great.” The seeker must look beyond achievements and ask how knowledge has changed the quality of their conduct.

When Possessiveness Looks Like Love

Human relationships are natural, yet Dr. Newton distinguishes loving another person from attempting to control that person's freedom. He uses marriage as an example: trying to reshape a partner into one's preferred image can turn a relationship into a form of bondage. Sharing a life and accepting responsibilities is one thing; claiming ownership over another person's personality and purpose is quite another.

The quality he associates with genuine love is acceptance. Respecting another person's past, present, and future; allowing them to be themselves; and supporting their life's purpose bring a relationship closer to unconditional love. When two people protect one another's freedom while cooperating in daily life, the relationship becomes a foundation for growth rather than confinement.

Attachment to Possessions, People, and Memories

Possessiveness is not confined to relationships. Money, gold, houses, and property can all serve practical purposes. The difficulty begins when we believe we are incomplete without them. The degree of distress we feel at their loss can reveal the inner hold they have acquired over us.

The speaker also invites us to move beyond narrow divisions such as 'my relatives' and 'my friends' toward a wider sense of human kinship. Repeatedly clinging to painful events from the past is another form of attachment. We can remember the past without continuously carrying its hurt into the present. The invitation is not to abandon responsibility, but to live responsibly while cultivating inward freedom.

Contentment Amid an Endless Chain of Desires

Once we acquire a house, we may want another; once we earn some money, we may seek more. Why, the speaker asks, does desire continue even after our needs have been met? However many houses we own, we can occupy only one at a time. His example of a simple place sufficient for spiritual practice points to the difference between comfort and contentment.

This is not a rejection of material necessities. Rather, it is an invitation to use what we have without allowing the endless demand for more to determine our peace. Recognizing a desire, examining the dissatisfaction beneath it, and distinguishing genuine need from attachment all belong to the work of inner purification.

The Subtle Message in Raktabija's Story

In the myth, each drop of Raktabija's blood that reaches the earth produces another Raktabija. Dr. Newton reads this as an image of ego's capacity to multiply. A subtle sense of 'I' may generate many reactions: one fulfilled desire leads to another, one form of recognition to a demand for more, and mental tendencies reinforce themselves.

He recalls the image of Kali preventing the blood from touching the ground. Within this interpretation, the story suggests noticing a tendency with awareness before it spreads into further reactions. The speaker also mentions simha kriya, the Vishuddha chakra, and obstructions of energy in the context of spiritual practice. These are presented as elements of his spiritual explanation, not as independently established medical claims.

Kaushiki Mahasaraswati: What Should We Seek?

The speaker describes Devi Shakti, within his spiritual framework, as capable of granting both worldly enjoyments and liberating wisdom. Yet the more important question is what we choose to ask for. Is the fulfillment of every desire our highest aim, or should we understand the ignorance that continually produces desires? He connects this inquiry to the wisdom aspect of Mahasaraswati and Kaushiki Mata during Navratri's final three days.

Using the sword of wisdom means seeing our inner bondage as an opportunity for self-inquiry rather than projecting it onto external enemies. Even an attachment that feels pleasant deserves examination if it limits freedom.

Avadhutas: Outer Appearance and Inner State

To explain why spiritual depth cannot be measured by clothing, wealth, or social standing, Dr. Newton speaks of avadhutas. He refers to Venkayya Swami, associated with the Nellore region, and reflects on how people sometimes dismiss an unconventional way of life as madness. What appears incomprehensible to ordinary social expectations may be understood differently within a life of intense devotion.

He also recalls his acquaintance with Chandrasekhar Sharma, whom he describes as a deeply devoted meditator. Someone who lives simply, perhaps even beside a road, cannot be judged by appearances alone. The unusual experiences he recounts belong to the speaker's personal recollections and spiritual descriptions.

The central lesson is to resist hasty judgments based on outward appearance. Humility, careful observation, and inner maturity matter more than visible status.

Nachiketa's Question: What Is Eternal?

Through the story of Nachiketa, the speaker turns toward liberating knowledge. The young seeker meets Yama, the lord of death, and refuses to be distracted by offers of wealth, comfort, and long life. He remains intent on understanding death and the nature of the Self. The story centers on discernment between what passes away and what is enduring.

Dr. Newton relates the teaching of the imperishable Self to Nachiketa Vidya and shares his spiritual understanding of the gross, subtle, and causal dimensions of existence. These are his interpretations of the Upanishadic story. At the heart of Nachiketa's inquiry lies the decision to value self-knowledge above temporary pleasures.

Inner Wounds: The Meaning We Give an Event

Toward the end of the teaching, the speaker asks listeners to examine how ego, possessiveness, fear, and emptiness remain active within us. People do not necessarily respond in identical ways to similar circumstances. For example, children who spend time away from their parents do not all develop the same emotional wound. How an experience is understood and what meaning a person assigns to it also matter.

He suggests that a childhood feeling of restricted freedom may later appear as an intense search for freedom, while the feeling of having gone unrecognized may manifest as a continuing need for approval. These are illustrative invitations to self-examination, not universal psychological diagnoses.

What words hurt us, and why? Why does one remark provoke a disproportionate reaction? Why might the absence of others' recognition leave us feeling empty? Honest attention to such questions can reveal bonds we did not know we were carrying.

On Navratri's seventh day, the destruction of asuras emerges not simply as an external battle but as an invitation to recognize recurring movements of the mind. Shumbha and Nishumbha's attraction and aversion, Raktabija's multiplying ego, the grip of possessiveness, and the dissatisfaction of endless desire all become matters for direct observation in daily life.

Meditation, awareness, trust, surrender, and discernment are the means Dr. Newton emphasizes. The true test of spiritual learning is whether what we understand begins to change how we live.

Divine Energy — Seeing Nature as Mother

The central purpose of Navratri practice is inner transformation. The battles between gods and demons in the Puranas, the speaker suggests, can be read not only as external events but also as symbols of the struggle among sattva, rajas, and tamas within us. Outer circumstances can invite us to examine our own tendencies. If we search for enemies only outside ourselves, we may miss what needs to change within.

He asks a pointed question: how can we worship the Divine Mother while polluting the very nature we regard as her manifestation? Cutting forests, discarding plastic into rivers and seas, and harming living creatures have consequences shared by the wider community. Offering reverence at a shrine while leaving its surroundings dirty exposes a contradiction in our worship.

Earth should not be regarded as mere soil, he says, but as Bhudevi, the Mother who supports us. Respect, love, and gratitude toward the mother who gave us birth can nurture our sense of reverence for the Divine Mother. Connection does not require physical proximity; it can be a lasting bond of the heart. The speaker worries that a generation constantly connected to technological devices may become disconnected from nature.

Invoking sarvam khalvidam brahma, he describes a unity underlying the diversity of creation. He mentions the minute structure of matter to evoke wonder at creation; this is part of his spiritual illustration, not independent scientific evidence for a metaphysical claim. Cleaning our own house while throwing rubbish into a neighbor’s space or the street contradicts the understanding of unity. If we truly recognize the same Self in everyone, our behavior toward others must reflect that recognition.

Anger — The Wounds Words Leave Behind

To illustrate the effects of angry speech, the speaker tells the story of a father and his son. The boy frequently lost his temper and spoke hurtful words. To make him aware of his anger, his father asked him to hammer a nail into a wall every time he became angry. On the first day the boy drove thirty-six nails. The wall made visible just how often anger took hold of him.

After some time, the father asked him to remove the nails one by one. The nails were gone, but the holes remained. Words spoken in anger can likewise leave wounds in another person’s heart. A word cannot be unsaid; even an apology may not entirely erase its effect. This is why awareness of speech matters.

The story leads back to the teaching of inner unity. To insult another person is not merely to hurt someone separate from ourselves; it also contradicts the understanding that the same consciousness is present in all. Knowing the doctrine of divine energy is one thing. Expressing that understanding through love, respect, and restraint in speech is another. The speaker compares a word to a sword: once uttered, it can pierce another person and leave a wound. He also emphasizes that a connection with the Divine Mother cannot remain merely theoretical; genuine respect and gratitude toward the mother who gave us birth can make that relationship a lived experience. Spiritual practice closes the distance between intellectual knowledge and experience.

The Flow of Time — Learning Through Experience and Moving Toward Liberation

We distinguish people by their bodies, names, qualities, and circumstances. The speaker offers another perspective: each person passes through a different journey of time and experience. He compares time to a river flowing in one direction. As rivers follow different bends before meeting the ocean, he describes the journey of the individual soul, in his spiritual framework, as moving toward liberation and Brahma-jnana.

From birth onward, the physical body changes, ages, and eventually dies. In discussing creation, preservation, and dissolution, he points to continual change in nature. Yet while the body undergoes its journey, the seeker’s inward search may move toward Self-realization.

The mind labels pleasant experiences as good and painful ones as bad. The speaker suggests that, from the standpoint of spiritual evolution, every experience may offer something to learn. Dreams, experiences during meditation, reading, self-study, and the company of seekers can each broaden understanding. This does not dismiss suffering; it invites us to learn from experience.

Reflecting on fear of death, darkness, and emptiness, he introduces another possibility: a person may hesitate not only before inner darkness but also before accepting the greatness of the light within. This leads to the teaching on Mahasaraswati.

Time, the Body, and the Journey of the Self — Further Explanations

The teacher begins with familiar expressions such as “your time is good” and “my time is not good.” Two people living in the same period can undergo very different experiences. He therefore speaks of time not merely as a number on a clock but as a journey each person experiences. Just as a river keeps flowing despite its bends, the seeker is invited not to lose sight of the spiritual destination amid life's changes.

He points to the changing functions of bodily cells and tissues with age and uses the imagery of creation, preservation, and dissolution. The teaching does not deny bodily decline; it invites inquiry while witnessing it: “Am I only this changing body?” Death is compared with darkness as an image of fear before the unknown. Lighting a lamp in a dark room becomes a metaphor for approaching that uncertainty with awareness.

Dream experiences, events in waking life, inner experiences during meditation, and understanding gained through study are presented as different opportunities in the same journey of learning. Rather than stopping at the mind's labels of “good” and “bad,” the seeker is encouraged to ask what each experience has revealed.

Mahasaraswati — Accepting the Light Within

It is relatively easy to hear Self-knowledge as an idea. Accepting aham brahmasmi — “I am Brahman” — as an experienced truth can be much harder. The speaker describes obstacles such as underestimating ourselves, doubting our capacities, and finding it difficult to accept the magnitude of our true nature.

Mahasaraswati is presented here as a symbol of knowledge, discernment, and inner illumination. Recognizing the light within is not an invitation to inflate the ego. It is an invitation to investigate our real nature. The Divine Mother’s form is not meant to remain only an object of external worship; the wisdom it signifies is to awaken within.

Exemplars Who Point Toward Wisdom

By asking whose portraits we keep in our homes, the teacher invites reflection on the people we choose as exemplars. Images of seekers and teachers such as the Buddha and Patriji are not merely decorations; they can remind us of the path of Self-knowledge they represent. He connects Mahasaraswati's grace not only with book learning but with the longing to experience one's true nature.

Musical skill and beautiful speech are also commonly described as gifts of Saraswati, he observes. In this teaching, however, his central emphasis is on higher wisdom—direct recognition of “I am the Self.” Respect for outward talents remains, but the purpose of Navratri's pursuit of knowledge is to recognize the light within.

Suratha and Samadhi — What We Ask For Reflects Our Inner State

Drawing on the Devi Mahatmya of the Markandeya Purana, the speaker tells of King Suratha, the merchant Samadhi, and Sage Medha. Suratha lost his kingdom after an attack by rival rulers and betrayal by his own ministers. Even after withdrawing to the forest, his mind remained attached to the kingdom. He worried about his people and wondered what had become of his former responsibilities.

Samadhi had been a successful merchant, but his wife and children deceived him and drove him away. Although they had hurt him, he continued to worry about their well-being. One man had lost a kingdom and the other his place in the family; both remained attached to what they had lost. This contradiction becomes the central question of the story.

They reached Sage Medha’s hermitage. The speaker invites attention to the significance of the names Suratha, Samadhi, and Medha, the last suggesting discernment. The sage heard their distress and showed them a path of practice. After intense spiritual practice, the Divine Mother appeared and invited them to ask for a boon. The speaker relates this part of the story to his own spiritual interpretation of meditation, the ajna chakra, and the power of intention.

Suratha asked to regain his kingdom. Samadhi asked instead for Self-knowledge, or Brahma-jnana, that would release him from the roots of his sorrow. Both received what they sought. Suratha regained his kingship and, according to the story, would become a Manu in a later age. Samadhi attained the knowledge and state of absorption he had sought.

The point is not simply to judge one request as superior. The speaker uses their different wishes to show how a person’s desires reflect the state of their inner development. Our intentions, speech, actions, and aims all reveal something about us. Recalling Patriji’s emphasis on speech, he encourages truthful and respectful words rather than abuse.

Practice, Intention, and Speech

In the story, Sage Medha does not immediately offer external solutions to Suratha and Samadhi. He directs the two troubled men toward spiritual practice. The teacher links this to his explanation of intention in meditation and the ajna chakra. He invokes images such as Kamadhenu, the wish-fulfilling tree, and the inexhaustible vessel to emphasize a question: “What are we asking for?” These are spiritual symbols within his interpretation.

Suratha's request to regain his kingdom and Samadhi's request for Self-knowledge reveal different aspirations. Rather than belittling either man, the story asks us to see how our wishes disclose the attachments still active within us. The teacher then turns to purity of speech: words, intentions, and desired blessings can all serve as material for self-examination. In this context he recalls Patriji's emphasis on the power and responsibility of speech.

The Seven Jnana Bhumikas — Seven Steps in the Inner Journey

The speaker compares the sapta jnana bhumikas, the seven stages of knowledge described by sages, to a ladder. They are not a tool for ranking other people. They are a framework for examining our own practice and seeing what the next step requires.

1. Shubhechha — A Sincere Desire for Knowledge

An event, a painful experience, or a teacher’s words may awaken a person to spiritual inquiry. On hearing of meditation, Self-knowledge, or Brahma-jnana, one may feel a genuine desire to discover that state and be free from sorrow. When this wish becomes a sincere intention rather than a passing remark, the speaker calls it shubhechha. It is the beginning of practice.

2. Vicharana — Inquiry into the Path

Once the desire arises, questions follow: How is Self-knowledge attained? How should one meditate? The seeker learns about meditation, self-study, and the company of good people; asks questions; and investigates the method of practice. The speaker names meditation, self-study, and satsanga as three important disciplines. Even practical questions about sitting and attending to the breath belong to this stage of inquiry.

3. Tanumanasi — Turning Understanding into Practice

Knowledge gained through inquiry now becomes action. Sitting steadily and comfortably, attending to the breath, and allowing the density of thoughts to lessen are aspects the speaker associates with tanumanasi. He illustrates the reciprocal influence of body and mind: mental distress can feel physically heavy, while bodily pain can disturb the mind. Making the mind “thin” means reducing its restlessness and approaching inner silence. Consistency in meditation, self-study, and good company matters here.

4. Sattvapatti — Inner Purification and the Beginning of Direct Insight

As practice deepens, the speaker describes increasing purity and meditative experience. In his spiritual vocabulary, the influence of rajas and tamas diminishes, sattva increases, and a practitioner may speak of purification of the subtle channels and movement of energy. He identifies sattvapatti as the fourth stage. The truth expressed by aham brahmasmi may become intellectually clear without yet being established as complete direct experience. He uses the term Brahmavid for a seeker at this level.

5. Asamsakti — Less Outer Attachment, More Inwardness

The fifth stage is asamsakti. As inward experiences become more important, attraction to wealth, display, and unnecessary distractions may decline. The speaker associates this stage with references to the ajna chakra and the “third eye.” A drop of water resting on a lotus leaf illustrates living in the world without clinging to it. Solitude can become an opportunity for practice rather than loneliness. The seeker may value silence and speak less. No one else can undertake the inward journey on one’s behalf. The speaker uses the title Brahmavidvara here.

6. Padarthabhavana — Intention, Experience, and Savikalpa Samadhi

The sixth stage is padarthabhavana. The speaker describes deeper meditative experiences, including states he calls savikalpa samadhi, while some questions or intentions may remain. Through the distinction among word, meaning, and object, he discusses the relationship between language, thought, intention, and experienced reality. References to energy, matter, and Einstein’s ideas are part of his spiritual analogies, not scientific proof that every spoken word literally becomes a physical object. He calls a seeker at this stage Brahmavidvariya.

7. Turiyaga — Nirvikalpa Samadhi and Witness-Consciousness

The seventh stage is turiyaga. The speaker associates it with the resolution of doubts that remain in savikalpa samadhi, with nirvikalpa samadhi, witness-consciousness, and the awakening of sahasrara. He describes those established in this state as helping others progress and guiding new masters. He uses terms such as Brahmavidvarishtha and Buddha in this discussion.

He then makes an important distinction: statements about karma and rebirth may sound different when expressed from the ordinary waking, dream, or deep-sleep states than when expressed from the standpoint of turiya. When reading the teachings of sages such as Ramana Maharshi, he urges listeners to consider the experiential standpoint from which the sage speaks. A declaration such as “there is no karma” should not be transferred uncritically into everyday life.

Taken together, these seven steps are not merely a list of names. They form a map of self-examination: from sincere intention to inquiry, from disciplined practice to purification, from non-attachment to deeper knowledge, and finally toward the witnessing state. The measure is not comparison with others but genuine transformation within oneself.

Non-Attachment, Silence, and the Solitary Nature of Practice

When explaining asamsakti, the teacher invokes the image of a drop of water resting on a lotus leaf. The point is to live in the world and carry out one's responsibilities without becoming inwardly bound to them. He describes a shift from fascination with outward things toward interest in inner practice. He also recommends some silence rather than constantly describing one's meditation experiences.

Meditation, he says, is an inward journey that each person must undertake. People may sit together to practice, but entering one's own inner experience remains personal. He also compares this with the individual nature of death. The teaching is not a blanket celebration of loneliness; it is a reminder that responsibility for self-inquiry cannot be handed to another person.

Subtle Distinctions Among the States of Knowledge

At the earlier steps, the seeker understands teachings and scriptures intellectually. The teacher then distinguishes meditation experiences, inner silence, and states of samadhi. In his explanation, savikalpa samadhi may still involve lingering doubts or distinctions, whereas nirvikalpa samadhi is associated with a more complete realization. He uses terms such as Brahmavid, Brahmavidvara, Brahmavidvariya, and Brahmavidvarishtha to express differences among these states; their meaning here must be read in the context of the original discourse.

In discussing padartha-bhavana, the teacher explores the relation between pada (word), artha (meaning), and padartha (thing or matter) from his spiritual perspective. He describes a relationship among intention, speech, and experienced reality. His references to matter and energy should be presented as illustrations or the speaker's interpretations, not as independent demonstrations of physics.

When speaking about the final stages, the teacher cautions that statements some sages make about karma and rebirth from the turiya state may appear contradictory to those at an ordinary level of experience. Referring to figures such as Ramtha and Ramana Maharshi, he encourages listeners not to detach a statement from the state of experience in which it is expressed. These are the teacher's spiritual interpretations, not claims established for every reader.

Surrender to the Divine Mother — Turning Knowledge into Experience

The teaching closes by inviting an attitude of surrender. To surrender is to open the inner being with the feeling of offering one’s energy to the Divine Mother. Gratitude toward the mother who gave us birth and remembrance of the Universal Mother are brought together as one movement of reverence. This is presented not simply as a ritual but as an inward practice of gratitude, humility, respect, and awareness.

Across Day Eight, the speaker links responsibility toward nature with care in speech, experience with Self-knowledge, examination of attachment with surrender. Understanding divine energy is meant to change how we live. That is the unifying invitation of this day’s teaching.

Knowing the Root Principle of Creation — The Oneness of Shakti, Consciousness, and Wisdom

The thread connecting the ninth day's teachings is directing energy through wisdom. Observing emotions, respecting nature, examining our relationship with our mother, joining outer pilgrimage with inner practice, and meditating with awareness in sacred settings all become different entrances to inner transformation. Stories, symbols, and personal experiences become meaningful when they awaken reflection and help us live with greater understanding.

Navratri Practice — Connecting with the Divine Mother's Energy

The nine days of Navratri offer an annual opportunity to dedicate oneself to spiritual practice. As the speaker explains, the purpose is not merely to learn about the Divine Mother's energy. It is to deepen one's connection with that energy, turn inward through meditation, and observe the changes unfolding within. Each period of practice can reveal a fresh understanding. With this background, he introduces principles for understanding and directing spiritual energy.

First Principle — Energy, Consciousness, and Knowledge

The reality worshipped as Jaganmata, Adi Shakti, or Divine Mother is described by the speaker in the language of shakti, or energy. In his spiritual account, meditation opens the way to experiencing energy; energy supports an awakening of consciousness; and the union of energy and consciousness gives rise to atma-jnana, knowledge of one's true nature. He is speaking here of higher Self-knowledge, rather than ordinary information or worldly learning.

He describes the stilling of thoughts in meditation, the perceived flow of universal energy, and experiences traditionally described as an awakening of Kundalini. These belong to the spiritual framework of his teaching. He then turns to the symbolic union of Prakriti and Purusha: nature represents energy, while Purusha represents consciousness. Shiva in deep stillness symbolizes consciousness; Parvati represents shakti. Ganesha and Kumara are understood within this symbolic language as expressions of wisdom.

The central teaching is not that energy and consciousness are unrelated concepts. Their integration, the speaker says, opens a deeper vision of knowledge.

Second Principle — Giving the Energy We Gather the Right Direction

Intensive practice may help a seeker gather energy, but gathering it is only one part of the work. The direction in which it is used matters just as much. The speaker refers to the significance of both Sharannavratri and the Navratri period associated with Sri Rama Navami as opportunities for dedicated practice.

He compares energy to water stored in a vast reservoir. Without a suitable channel, the water may breach its banks and cause damage; directed carefully, it becomes useful. The same principle applies to attention: where attention goes, energy flows.

When attention continually returns to grievances, loneliness, painful memories, and complaints, those patterns may become increasingly dominant. Thoughts, speech, actions, and emotional reactions all occupy our time and attention. The invitation is to recognize recurring mental habits and consciously choose a more constructive direction for one's energy.

Third Principle — Inner Realization and Accomplishment in the World

The speaker distinguishes two complementary uses of energy. Turned inward and upward in spiritual practice, it supports jnana-siddhi, the realization of wisdom. Directed outward toward meaningful work, it supports karya-siddhi, the successful completion of a task. Neither need exclude the other; what matters is the purpose served by outward action.

Hanuman provides the illustration. He already possessed tremendous strength, but that strength acquired a clear direction when it was dedicated to Rama-karya, service to Rama's great purpose. The abilities of Sugriva, Nila, Jambavan, and others came together for a common undertaking. Great work, in this account, is not simply a display of one person's power: it can be a shared effort in the service of something larger.

In ordinary life, the speaker advises replacing endless gossip, fault-finding, and complaints with satsanga, the company of people who encourage understanding and growth. Satsanga is more than social gathering; it is an atmosphere in which people share what they have learned. Rather than dwelling on another person's faults, we can practice noticing and appreciating at least one good quality in them.

The principle thus has two sides: employ energy in worthwhile work in the world, and deepen Self-knowledge within.

Fourth Principle — Emotions and Obstructions to the Flow of Energy

Having explained the gathering and direction of energy, the speaker examines what obstructs its flow. When a water pipe becomes blocked, water may spill out and leave the surrounding area muddy. Just as a plumber identifies and repairs the blockage, a seeker needs to investigate the emotional patterns that interrupt inner clarity. But the work of observing one's own reactions cannot simply be delegated to someone else; awareness itself must become the guide.

Some emotions are not born in a single moment. Fear, anger, grief, shame, and the expectation of rejection may have been carried since childhood and may return in familiar forms. Within his spiritual worldview, the speaker also relates some such tendencies to impressions from previous lives. He uses the expression emotion as energy in motion: anger and fear have force, yet their direction may not serve us well.

Gratitude and joy can leave a person feeling open and energetic, while fear, shame, and inferiority can feel draining. The talk also refers to purported research measuring emotional energy. The precise author and scientific validity of those measurements are not established by the source, so this should be read as a claim mentioned in the discourse, not as a verified scientific result.

There are two familiar responses to difficult feelings: suppress them, or discharge them onto others. The speaker proposes a third possibility. Notice the emotion, name it, and investigate where its repeated pattern may be coming from. Fears that we will not be accepted, that everyone will leave, or that we are inadequate can quietly occupy our attention. A water tank with a tiny leak eventually empties; similarly, unexamined emotional habits can gradually exhaust our sense of vitality.

He gives the example of tension at home. Even kind words or a friendly smile may be misunderstood by another person. One response is to remain wounded by the misunderstanding; another is to ask what the experience might teach us. The speaker interprets such situations through the spiritual ideas of karma and runanubandha, karmic relationship. His practical point is the importance of how we interpret an event. The event may remain unchanged, but our response can shift when our understanding changes.

He describes love, joy, peace, gratitude, kindness, compassion, enthusiasm, and optimism as life-supporting qualities. Persistent anger, fear, and hatred, by contrast, can leave us depleted. His illustration of forty days of practice seeming to vanish in one outburst of anger is best understood as a warning about losing awareness, not as a literal measurement of energy.

The Vision of Wisdom — Seeing the Lotus in the Mud

To explain a change of perspective, the speaker tells a story about a teacher and disciple visiting a muddy pond. Beautiful lotuses of pink, red, white, and yellow bloom there. Someone who notices only the mud sees an unpleasant pond. Someone who notices the flowers sees beauty. Looking still more deeply, one recognizes that the mud itself nourished the lotuses.

In the same way, difficult experiences and misunderstandings can sometimes become occasions for learning. The speaker calls the capacity to recognize and appreciate such learning Fully Appreciating Awareness. This does not mean denying pain. It means remaining open to understanding even in the presence of pain.

He also interprets emotional patterns through the language of chakras and subtle energy. Rejection, separation, and emotional hurt are associated in his account with obstructions around the Anahata or heart centre; difficulty expressing feelings is related symbolically to other centres. He also mentions Manipura, Svadhishthana, and Muladhara, along with meditation, purification of subtle channels, Vishuddhi, and movement toward Ajna. These are concepts within the speaker's yogic framework, not medical diagnoses.

When discomfort or bodily pain arises during meditation, the speaker discourages immediately concluding, “I must have done something wrong; this is punishment.” He instead encourages awareness rather than fear. Persistent or severe physical symptoms, however, still warrant appropriate medical assessment; spiritual interpretation does not replace healthcare.

An insight is a new way of seeing an experience from within. A seven-storey building looks one way from ground level and quite another from its upper floors. Likewise, a narrow viewpoint may see only problems, while a wider vision can place those same events in a larger context.

Durgamasura and Shakambari — Knowledge, Compassion, and the Three Gunas

The speaker interprets demons such as Mahishasura, Shumbha and Nishumbha, Chanda and Munda, Dhumralochana, and Raktabija as symbols of rajas, tamas, and their manifestations within human life. He then recounts the story of Durgamasura, who hides the Vedas. With knowledge seemingly lost, drought and suffering spread across the earth. The Vedas here symbolize wisdom: where understanding is absent, fear and sorrow grow; as Self-knowledge deepens, the speaker teaches, joy becomes more accessible.

Moved by the suffering of living beings, the Divine Mother appears as Shakambari. In the story as the speaker tells it, the tears flowing from the Mother's compassion become nourishing plants, vegetables, and fruit-bearing trees. Compassion takes the form of sustenance. Durgamasura is subsequently defeated. The narrative is offered not simply as an external miracle but as a symbol of knowledge overcoming ignorance and compassion overcoming indifference.

The aim of these stories, the speaker adds, is not merely to overcome rajas and tamas. Ultimately, the seeker must also move beyond attachment to sattva and toward a state described as trigunatita, beyond the three gunas. This returns the teaching to its opening principles: dedicated practice gives energy direction, supporting purposeful work outwardly and wisdom inwardly.

Mother Nature — Recognizing the Divine Mother in Creation

The Divine Mother, the speaker reminds listeners, should not be confined to a form in a shrine. Trees, animals, and the many expressions of life can be regarded as manifestations of Prakriti Mata, Mother Nature. To see nature as a living presence rather than merely a storehouse of resources is to approach it with gratitude and respect.

To explore this relationship, he turns to the most familiar image of motherhood: one's own mother. She may live in the same home, and yet there may be great emotional distance. Conversely, she may live far away while remaining close in one's heart. The question is therefore not simply physical proximity, but the quality of the inner relationship.

Judgment, criticism, and refusal to accept another person can widen that distance. The speaker's central phrase is that judgment separates us. In his spiritual interpretation, learning to see and accept one's mother more fully can also influence one's relationship with the Divine Mother. He relates outward human relationships to their inward spiritual reflections.

This invitation to self-examination should not be taken as a demand that every family relationship become close regardless of circumstances. It does not negate necessary personal boundaries or safety. Its focus is the possibility of recognizing the judgments and emotional distance within ourselves.

Pilgrimage and the Inner Journey — Transformation Matters More Than Travel

Asked about pilgrimages, the speaker does not dismiss visits to the Ganga, temples, or sacred places. Those with the opportunity and resources may go, he says. But meditation and inner practice at those places are crucial. Travel alone does not automatically transform the mind; practice undertaken at home can also become an inner pilgrimage.

He illustrates this through a story set after the Mahabharata war. The Pandavas invite Sri Krishna to accompany them on pilgrimage. Unable to join them, Krishna gives them a pumpkin to take as his representative. They are to immerse it in the rivers they visit and place it at the sites of worship before bringing it home.

According to the story as recounted in the discourse, the Pandavas carry the pumpkin for roughly forty days. Upon their return, Krishna asks that it be cooked and served. The people who eat it vomit. The Pandavas are astonished that something taken to so many sacred places could have this effect. Krishna's response points to the lesson: just as visiting holy places did not change the pumpkin's nature, travelling to holy places does not by itself transform a person's inner life. This is an illustrative story from the discourse, not a historically verified event.

The lesson is not that pilgrimage is worthless. Rather, an outward journey gains depth when accompanied by inward practice. The speaker also connects Kashi, Manasarovar, Kailasa, Jyotirlingas, and Shakti sites with inner spiritual meanings. The outer pilgrimage and the inner pilgrimage can enrich one another.

Meditation at Sacred Places — The Speaker's Kanchi Kamakshi Experience

After emphasizing the inner journey, the speaker describes the special significance he attributes to temples. While teaching in Tamil Nadu, he visited the Kanchi Kamakshi temple. He mentions traditional accounts of the intensity of the Goddess's presence there and the role attributed to Adi Shankaracharya in making that energy accessible to devotees. These are presented as spiritual beliefs shared in the discourse.

While meditating in the temple, he reports an inner experience in which Kamakshi appeared with a parrot and seemed to invite him to sit upon it. He explicitly treats this as an experience on a subtle level, inviting symbolic understanding. At the time, he says, he knew little of the Goddess's association with parrots. Afterwards he noticed parrots around the temple and in depictions of the deity, and later read accounts connecting parrots with the tradition.

He further recalls reading that sages and yogis may be present near Kamakshi in the forms of parrots. This is a spiritual tradition he reports having encountered, not a claim independently verified here. Nor does he suggest that every meditator must have the same experience.

He also mentions Srisailam and encourages visiting sacred places and meditating there when circumstances allow, while keeping the inner journey central. He speaks of prana-pratishtha, temple architecture, sacred geometry, and symbolic connections between pyramids, Mount Meru, and Sri Chakra. In his view, such settings may support meditation. Yet he advises against forcing an image of the deity into the mind: quiet sitting and inner silence matter more than trying to manufacture an experience.

Physical Difficulties and Karmic Lessons — Practising Joy with Awareness

Toward the end of the session, a participant asks about health difficulties. The speaker responds from his spiritual perspective, suggesting that persistent challenges may be approached as karmic lessons rather than punishments. He encourages letting go of fixation on the past and practising joy, contentment, and awareness in the present. His expression practice joy points toward loosening the condition that happiness is possible only when everything goes as planned.

He also notes that people can have blind spots—patterns they cannot readily recognize on their own—and that support from others may help them see more clearly. This observation is not a medical explanation for illness.

The questioner mentions recurring PCOD or menstrual-cycle difficulties. The speaker suggests seeking support, counselling, or guidance from experienced people to examine issues that may be hard to notice alone. This is a description of the spiritual and personal guidance in the talk, not a substitute for clinical care.

Medical distinction: There is no established medical basis for asserting that PCOD or other physical diseases are caused by karma. Ongoing or recurrent symptoms deserve appropriate assessment and treatment from qualified healthcare professionals. Meditation and spiritual practice may be personally meaningful, but they do not replace medical care.

Conclusion — Bringing Wisdom into Everyday Life

The thread connecting the ninth day's teachings is directing energy through wisdom. Observing emotions, respecting nature, examining our relationship with our mother, joining outer pilgrimage with inner practice, and meditating with awareness in sacred settings all become different entrances to inner transformation. Stories, symbols, and personal experiences become meaningful when they awaken reflection and help us live with greater understanding.

Vijaya Dashami — Recognizing Divine Power and Succeeding in Sadhana Through Self-Knowledge

The true test remains the same as on earlier days: whether the understanding begins to change how energy is received, held, and used in daily life.

Knowing About Shakti and Knowing Shakti

On the tenth day of Navratri, Vijaya Dashami, Dr. Newton Kondaveti draws a clear distinction that runs through the entire teaching. Knowing about Devi Shakti is different from knowing Shakti itself. Knowing about Atma Jnana is different from knowing the Self. Knowing about Truth is different from knowing Truth.

Whenever the word “about” enters, the mind moves into analysis, theory, and concepts. The nine days of Navratri are not meant for that. They are a favorable time for practice, just as the rainy season is a favorable time for a farmer to sow. The flow of Shakti is especially supportive during these days. That is why meditators regard Dasara as a great festival of sadhana.

Without understanding the inner meaning, rituals remain only ceremonies. Our ancestors performed them with understanding; merely repeating the outer form without the meaning leaves us unclear about why we do them.

The Seven Principles of Energy and Consciousness

Dr. Newton recalls seven principles shared earlier and returns to the first of them. Energy is present throughout nature and comes to us in many ways — through sound, through food, through the senses. Yet the primary and most important way a meditator receives the universal energy is through meditation.

When this energy fills us and we become, as he puts it, “masses of energy,” consciousness becomes known. Energy and consciousness first appear as two. In reality they are one — two different expressions of the same. When the Kundalini Shakti rises and meets consciousness, the two become one. This is the principle of Ardhanarishvara: Parvati and Shiva are not separate. From their union arises knowledge.

These three — Shakti, Chaitanya (consciousness), and Jnana — unfold in every sincere meditator. During the favorable conditions of Navratri, when practice is steady, we receive more of the universal energy. Through that energy, consciousness awakens; through that consciousness, knowledge becomes available. The whole journey of sadhana is for Atma Jnana — to know who we are, what our purpose is, and how to live it.

Containing the Energy and Directing It

The energy that awakens within us is the universal energy, the Kundalini Shakti that has been lying latent. It is not to be wasted. There are many leakages through which energy is misused. The seeker must learn to contain it, to hold it as a reservoir, and then to put it to right use for a higher purpose.

Dr. Newton gives the example of Hanuman, whose strength was used for Rama’s work — a grand purpose. Whatever the awakened energy is, it is meant for such a purpose, not for small or scattered ends.

The energy moves in two directions. When it remains inward, it is associated with the feminine principle — feminine energy. When it moves outward, it expresses as masculine energy. During the nine days of Navratri the meditator stays more inward, experiencing the energy directly. On Vijaya Dashami that contained energy begins to be used outwardly. Vijaya Dashami is the beginning of victories.

Two Directions: Jnana Siddhi and Karya Siddhi

The energy that has been activated through practice is used in two ways. When it rises upward within, it brings Jnana Siddhi — Atma Jnana, Brahma Jnana. When it is directed outward into the gross world, it supports Karya Siddhi — the successful completion of the work we have undertaken.

Dr. Newton recalls the story of the Pandavas. After their period of exile they retrieved the weapons they had hidden near the Jammi tree. From Vijaya Dashami onward their victories began. The outer story carries an inner meaning: the energy gathered and contained during sadhana is now brought out for the work that remains.

In the outer world many events and situations continue. The energy directed outward helps us meet them skillfully. The energy directed inward brings the knowledge of the Self. Both are necessary. One is not a rejection of the other.

Stories, the Three Worlds, and Going Beyond Cause and Effect

All the Puranic stories, Dr. Newton reminds us, carry deep inner meaning and knowledge. What appears in the gross world has its template in the subtle (energetic, astral) world, and the root causes lie in the causal body. The state beyond cause and effect — the mahākāraṇa — is reached only in meditation, with the help of Shakti.

He recalls the familiar progression: jāgrat, svapna, suṣupti, and beyond them turiya. Only when one crosses into turiya do the bindings of cause and effect begin to fall away.

Tamo and Rajo Gunas as the Inner Asuras

Tamo guna and Rajo guna obstruct the free flow of energy. When these two dominate, we behave like asuras. Sattva must predominate. Even a small presence of Tamo and Rajo remains, but the percentage of Sattva needs to be high for the pure sattvic state.

Meditators naturally carry more energy. With steady practice, laziness and negativity diminish. Ahankara, mamakara, raga and dvesha gradually lose their force. The battle described in the Navratri stories — the slaying of Mahishasura and the other asuras — is precisely this battle against the obstructing gunas.

When energy flows strongly, it clears the blocks, just as water under pressure clears a blocked pipe. Emotional blocks, memory blocks, old fears, anger and sorrow have solidified within us. Intense practice during the favorable period of Navratri helps these clear. This is the seventh point he mentioned earlier: nāḍī-maṇḍala śuddhi happens in a special way, the obstructions are removed, and the energy sets itself in a clear direction.

Victory over the Ten Indriyas and the Symbol of Ravana

Another inner meaning of Daśamī is victory over the ten indriyas. When Shakti is established within, control over the senses becomes natural.

In North India the burning of the Ravana effigy is performed on Vijaya Dashami. The ten heads are dismantled and dissolved. Watching that image can serve as a reminder to dismantle, within ourselves, the ten aspects that keep the ego expanded.

The Inner Meaning of the Festival

Vijaya Dashami is not merely the end of nine days of outer celebration. It marks the point where the energy that has been gathered, purified, and contained is ready to be used — both for the knowledge that frees and for the action that serves. The obstructing gunas have been addressed, the blocks have begun to clear, and the indriyas are brought under the influence of the awakened energy.

The invitation is practical. Do not treat the days of Navratri only as ritual. Use the favorable flow of Shakti for real practice. Learn to receive energy primarily through meditation. Recognize that energy and consciousness are not two. Contain what awakens. Allow the gunas that obstruct to be reduced. Direct the energy, when the time comes, toward a purpose larger than personal gain. Then the outer festival becomes an inner victory.

The true test remains the same as on earlier days: whether the understanding begins to change how energy is received, held, and used in daily life.

Meditation as Witnessing

Meditation is a process to observe and to stay aware and mindful. We need to stay in the witness state where we become the watcher. We witness all the changes that occur in our breathing as the mind becomes empty and silent. The breath rate comes down naturally. Meditation is an internalized state. We withdraw all our senses from the external world, just as a tortoise withdraws its limbs. Whatever is happening outside is none of our concern right now.

Observe the Observer

Right now you are observing your breath. The breath is slowing down and the mind is getting emptied. You are witnessing everything. While you are observing, also observe the observer. We don’t need to control anything — not thoughts, not breathing. Just give up the control and be in the most natural state. Become the watcher of the watcher.

Journey from Gross to Subtle

Meditation is a journey from the gross to the subtle, from the subtle to the subtlest. It is an inner journey. Keep noticing in these subtler states of consciousness whatever is happening within you. Reach the deeper place within you where time and space dissolve, beyond body and mind.

Water Analogy — States of Consciousness

Just as water (H2O) exists in different states: at lowest temperatures it is ice (solid), then liquid, then vapor at highest temperature. A meditator experiences these states. We reach the subtlest states like vapor. These correspond to Jagrat (waking, using all senses), Swapna (conscious dreaming, visions at the third eye), Sushupti (deep absorbed state where one is not even aware of watching the breath), and Turiya (transcendental state). In a certain state of consciousness you transcend all limitations of body and mind. Pain can disappear. You tap into atomic-like energy within.

Watch the Watcher — Multiple Levels

We need to become aware of our awareness. Whoever is watching the breath, watching the stillness, we have to become the watcher to the watcher. We go on multiple levels: observer of the observer. The more levels we reach, the more we enter Turiya and transcendental states. There are more beautiful states of consciousness that a meditator experiences.

Gandhi Jayanti — Inspiration and the Bhagavad Gita

Today is Gandhi Jayanti. Gandhi means nonviolence. He always carried the Bhagavad Gita with him. While walking, he had a stick in one hand and the Bhagavad Gita in the other. He is an inspiration not only to this country but to the world. Even if there are controversies, we should focus on what we can learn from anyone.

Raghupati Raghava Rajaram — The Message

The Gandhi Jayanti celebration was begun with the song Raghupati Raghava Rajaram. His entire message is in these two lines. Rama brought a wonderful balance greater than anyone. Truthfulness, standing by one’s word. In Kaliyuga he brought that knowledge like a bridge. “Ishwar Allah tero naam” — whether Ishwara, Allah, Krishna, or Buddha, all are names of the same divine principle. Buddha and Angulimala

Buddha did not speak about nonviolence in words. He demonstrated it. Angulimala was a person who frightened everyone. Buddha went to him courageously. When Angulimala told him to stop, Buddha said, “I have already stopped; you have not stopped.” Angulimala became afraid and asked for that courage. Finally Angulimala became a Buddhist monk. Gandhi’s Ashram — 11 Principles

Gandhi called those who wanted to fight for freedom to the ashram. About 40 people came. He stated 11 guiding principles. First is truth — one must remain with truth under all circumstances. Second is nonviolence — it is not shouting “I am with truth.” Violence begins in thought, then comes in speech, then in behavior. Third is brahmacharya — there should be no other attention. Everyone should work and do their own work. He himself cleaned his room. There should be no fear. Even if death comes one should stand without fear. There should be equality toward all religions.

First We Must Change

Gandhi clearly said in his books that he made a complete effort to follow the values he spoke of, but that he also failed at times — he said this without any hesitation.

Pitru Paksha — Gratitude to Ancestors

Introduction to Jivatma–Paramatma Tattva

Today and tomorrow the theme is Jivatma–Paramatma. We should come to know about these two words: jiva–deva, jivatma–paramatma. First we should know the knowledge; later we obtain the experience. On the occasion of Navaratri and Vijaya Dashami the Seemollanghan custom that exists in India was mentioned. Seemollanghan — Crossing Limitations

Seemollanghan means beyond the comfort zone. Crossing the village boundaries and returning is the custom. As jivatma we remain in the body and place many limitations upon ourselves. We ourselves create the limits of “I am only this much, I cannot do this.” Paramatma tattva is unlimited. It keeps removing our limits. When ahankara comes it works to shatter it.

The Eagle Chick Story — Coming Out of the Comfort Zone

The mother eagle raises the chick well. She places it softly in the nest. The chick wants to remain there forever. It thinks it is safe. The mother removes the feathers and leaves so that thorns become visible. The chick does not listen. It is afraid. The mother pushes it out with love itself. While falling, the chick remembers that it has wings and begins to fly. Our life is the same. We must come out of the comfort zone.

Paramatma Tattva — Removing Limits

Paramatma tattva always keeps removing limits. When ahankara comes it shows it. If ahankara arises that we were born beautiful, it makes wrinkles appear. If we think we have a strong body, it declines in old age. It also cuts the bond of thinking we will remain in the body forever. We think “I am this, I am that.” That ahankara itself is the limit. Paramatma keeps breaking it.

Beyond Comfort Zone — Practice

Today we should hold this point: beyond the comfort zone. Do not think “I am only this much, I cannot do more than this.” If we walk 500 metres, try another half kilometre. Even if we say we are unable to meditate, sit in the beyond-comfort-zone. If done for 10, 20, 40 days we will be able to cross the limits. Paramatma tattva means unlimitedness. The great ocean has no limits.

Desires, Needs, and Sukha–Duhkha

Desires arise from the mind. If the desire is fulfilled there is happiness; if not fulfilled there is disappointment, sinking, and even depression. There is nothing wrong in desiring. Creation began with the will of the Divine. But everything should happen in connection. When meditation practice is begun clarity comes. What is beneficial and what is not beneficial becomes known. Eating ten jalebis is unnecessary for the body. One is enough. If happiness is desired, unhappiness also comes along with it. They are twins. This discrimination knowledge comes to the meditation practitioner.

Shreyas and Preyas

With the discrimination of buddhi we can recognize what is shreyas and what is preyas. Ordinarily we run after what is pleasing. Temporary happiness comes, and unhappiness also comes along with it. What is beneficial (shreyas) should be chosen. They never trouble us. “Loka samasta sukhino bhavantu” is a beneficial sankalpa. Paramatma tattva always supports what is shreyas, because it is in connection with that tattva.

The Goal of the Meditation Practitioner

In meditation practitioners external desires decrease. Distractions decrease. They come out of the cycle of sukha and duhkha. They are able to go inward. The more inward we become, the more we are able to remain in the state of paramananda and brahmananda beyond sukha and duhkha. As jivatma we are acting in a drama. Paramatma tattva is the goal. Everything is that Paramatma tattva.

Trees Too Are Paramatma Tattva
