The Sri Ganesha series took us beyond the familiar stories of Ganesha's avatars to explore the deeper tattva, or spiritual principles, represented by them. Drawing primarily from the Mudgala Purana, the sessions encouraged us to recognise that the asuras and their stories can also represent tendencies within our own minds.

The eight avatars were connected with qualities such as jealousy, pride, delusion, greed, anger, desire, possessiveness and ego. Each story offered an opportunity to understand how these tendencies influence our thoughts and actions, and how they can be transformed into qualities such as humility, contentment, generosity, discernment and universal love.

The teachings also invited us to look differently at obstacles. A difficulty in life need not always be seen as something to resist or remove immediately. It can become an opportunity to pause, examine ourselves and recognise what needs to change within us.

Ultimately, the series reminded us that understanding Ganesha Tattva is not merely about knowing the stories or worshipping the forms. It is about awakening the wisdom, humility and awareness that they point towards.

The sessions explored how conflict often arises not simply from what is said, but from the feelings, judgments and unmet needs behind our words. Taking responsibility for a mistake does not require harsh self-judgment; it requires understanding, acceptance and a willingness to respond differently.

The larger invitation was to make peaceful communication a way of life, allowing understanding and compassion to find expression in our everyday relationships.

Navratri: From Celebration to Inner Transformation

The Navratri sessions looked past outward celebration to the spiritual meaning of the nine days — Shakti and consciousness, the Divine Mother's energy, surrender, and the awakening of wisdom within.

Stories from the Devi Mahatmya treated asuras as inner tendencies: desire, anger, jealousy, attachment, pride, and ego. The Mother's battles became a map of the inner work needed to outgrow our limits.

The nine days also framed iccha, kriya, and jnana shakti — will, action, and wisdom — moving from purifying tendencies toward discernment and deeper awareness. Navratri is not only an outer festival; it is time for meditation, practice, and meeting the Divine within.

The Navratri sessions invited us to look beyond the outward celebrations and rituals to understand the spiritual significance of the nine days. The teachings explored Shakti and consciousness, the Divine Mother's energy, surrender and the awakening of wisdom within.

Through stories from the Devi Mahatmya, the various asuras were examined as representations of tendencies that can operate within us, including desire, anger, jealousy, attachment, pride and ego. The battles between the Divine Mother and these forces became a way of understanding the inner work required to overcome our own limitations.

The nine days also offered a framework for reflecting on the three fundamental powers: iccha shakti (will), kriya shakti (action) and jnana shakti (wisdom). The journey moved from recognising and purifying inner tendencies towards developing discernment, surrender and deeper awareness.

An important message was that Navratri is not merely a festival to be observed externally. It is an opportunity to dedicate ourselves to meditation and spiritual practice, direct our energy towards self-knowledge and experience the Divine principles within us.

The same awareness shapes how we earn and spend money, use our time, and care for our bodies. We can question beliefs about scarcity and self-worth, direct attention toward what matters, and support well-being through nourishment, movement, and rest.

The Wisdom of Devi Navratri: Nine Days of Inner Awakening

9 Days · Dr. Newton Kondaveti

Navratri is often celebrated through devotion, rituals, music and festivities — but what lies beneath these traditions? Across nine classes, Dr. Newton Kondaveti invited participants to look beyond outward celebration and explore the spiritual wisdom woven into the festival.

Through Purana stories, yogic concepts and practical reflections, the series unfolded as a journey of inner awareness, transformation and surrender — from understanding Shakti and consciousness, to recognising Deva and Asura tendencies within, to carrying that wisdom into everyday living.

Navratri is often celebrated through devotion, rituals, music and festivities. But what lies beneath these traditions? During the nine days of Devi Navratri, Brahmarshi Chakravarthy Dr. Newton Kondaveti invited participants to look beyond the external celebrations and explore the spiritual wisdom woven into the festival. Through stories from the Puranas, yogic concepts and practical reflections, the sessions unfolded as a journey of inner awareness, transformation and surrender.

From Festival to Inner Practice

The opening classes explored why festivals carry a deeper purpose. Dr. Newton reflected on Bathukamma, the symbolism of the Divine Mother and the principle of creation, sustenance and dissolution. Even the immersion of a deity's form was presented as a reminder that all forms eventually dissolve, inviting us to reflect on the gradual dissolution of the ego and our limited sense of "I" and "mine".

The significance of the nine nights was also explored as an opportunity for inward practice. Rather than allowing our energy to remain scattered through everyday distractions, participants were encouraged to direct their attention inward through meditation and self-inquiry.

Understanding Shakti and Consciousness

A central thread throughout the series was the relationship between Shakti and Chaitanya, energy and consciousness. Dr. Newton explained how the Divine Mother is represented through different forms, including Durga, Lakshmi, Saraswati and Kali, while emphasising that these forms point towards the same underlying energy.

He also distinguished between enjoyment and celebration. Enjoyment often depends on fulfilling a desire, whereas true celebration arises from within and does not depend on external circumstances. The nine days were presented as an opportunity to experience this inner joy through meditation, service and Self-knowledge.

An important practice introduced during the sessions was Devi Samarpana, surrender to the Divine Mother. Participants were encouraged to offer their ego, difficulties and aspirations with humility, while recognising the presence of Jaganmata in themselves, other people and nature. Total Acceptance was another key practice: accepting ourselves, others and our present circumstances without giving up the possibility of growth.

The Deva and Asura Within Us

The classes then turned to the symbolic meaning of the devas and asuras. Rather than viewing them only as characters in Puranic stories, Dr. Newton invited participants to recognise them as tendencies within human beings. Harmony, clarity and awareness reflect our divine nature, while anger, pride, possessiveness and excessive desire can create inner discord.

The three gunas — Tamas, Rajas and Sattva — were discussed in this context. Participants were encouraged to observe which qualities influence their thoughts and actions and consciously cultivate greater balance, clarity and service.

The three stages of Navratri were connected to three forms of Shakti: Mahadurga, representing the will to overcome obstacles; Mahalakshmi, nurturing positive qualities and inner resources; and Mahasaraswati, guiding the seeker towards discernment and Self-knowledge. Together, they represent a progression from overcoming limitations to developing noble qualities and awakening wisdom.

Discovering the Stories Within the Stories

Several sessions explored the deeper meanings of stories from the Devi Mahatmyam. The story of Rambha and Karambha, for instance, was used to examine the two currents within us: worldly desires and the longing for liberation. Dr. Newton encouraged participants not to suppress desires blindly, but to understand what lies beneath them and recognise the habits and impressions that continue to influence our responses.

The six emissaries of Mahishasura were connected with six inner adversaries: Kama (desire), Krodha (anger), Lobha (greed), Moha (attachment), Mada (pride) and Matsarya (jealousy). The story of Mahishasuramardini was explored as a symbolic account of overcoming these tendencies through awareness and spiritual practice.

The later classes examined Shumbha, Nishumbha and other asuras as representations of attachment to pleasure, aversion to pain, self-deception and the multiplying nature of ego. The sword of the Divine Mother became a symbol of wisdom and discernment, reminding participants that the real battle is within.

Wisdom in Everyday Living

As the series progressed, the teachings extended beyond meditation and mythology into everyday conduct. Participants were encouraged to recognise nature as an expression of the Divine Mother and to reflect on whether their actions truly demonstrated respect for the Earth and other living beings.

Dr. Newton also highlighted the lasting impact of words through the story of a father who asked his angry son to hammer nails into a wall whenever he lost his temper. Although the nails could later be removed, the holes remained. The story offered a simple reminder that words spoken in anger can leave lasting impressions, making awareness and compassion in communication essential.

The final class brought together the relationship between Shakti, consciousness and Jnana, or wisdom. It explored the importance of directing the energy gathered through spiritual practice towards a higher purpose. The distinction between Jnana Siddhi, the realisation of wisdom, and Karya Siddhi, accomplishment in the world, highlighted that inner growth and meaningful action need not be separate.

A Journey Beyond the Nine Days

Across the nine classes, one message remained constant: the deeper purpose of Navratri is not simply to celebrate the Divine Mother, but to recognise and experience the principles she represents within ourselves and throughout creation.

Through meditation, self-inquiry, surrender and conscious action, the festival becomes more than an annual observance. It becomes an invitation to transform the way we think, relate, speak and live, carrying the wisdom of these nine days into every day that follows.

When the Universe Has Other Plans

2 October 2026 · Hyderabad · Ahimsa Dhyana Yagnam

A journey to Hyderabad, an unexpected car breakdown and an unplanned cab ride. What seemed like an inconvenient start to the day soon took an unexpected turn, bringing together people and circumstances in a way no one could have anticipated. Along the way, a simple conversation opened the door to a meaningful connection, reminding us that some of life's most significant moments are never planned. Join Harika as she recounts this unexpected journey and the beautiful encounter that gave it a deeper meaning.

The morning of 2nd October was bright and pleasant, and we were looking forward to another journey with Dr. Newton and Dr. Lakshmi. Travelling with them is always special. It is the time spent together, the conversations and the many little opportunities to learn along the way that make these journeys so enriching. This time, we were headed to Hyderabad for the Ahimsa Dhyana Yagnam, where Sir and Ma'am were scheduled to conduct a session.

We had driven from Vikarabad to Hyderabad to pick them up. Everything had gone smoothly, and the car had been running perfectly throughout the journey. When we reached to pick up Sir and Ma'am, the sun was beginning to get a little stronger. Just as they settled into the car and we were ready to leave, the vehicle decided to play a little game with us. It simply refused to start.

I remember feeling quite annoyed and, more than anything, apologetic. We had to ensure that Sir and Ma'am reached the venue on time, and here we were, suddenly stranded with no explanation for what had happened. The car had been running perfectly right up until that moment, all the way from Vikarabad. We tried repeatedly, but the car wouldn't respond. It was difficult to understand how a vehicle that had brought us all the way without a hitch could suddenly stop working. It was beginning to get hotter, and our frustration and anxiety were also rising.

Dr. Newton, however, remained remarkably calm. While the rest of us were trying to figure out what had gone wrong, he simply said, "There is a reason for everything. Don't worry. We have our plans, but this has its own plan."

Ultimately, with no other option, we booked a cab so that we could reach the venue on time. I was still annoyed at how a perfectly planned journey could take such an abrupt and uncertain turn.

However, little did we know that this unexpected change of vehicle would bring someone into our lives who was about to experience a change of his own.

A Conversation We Hadn't Planned

When we got into the cab, the driver, Anand, was already on the phone. He sounded frustrated and deeply troubled by something happening in his life. His voice was raised, and the conversation seemed intense. We had just been through our own unexpected disruption, and the tension in his voice added to the unsettled mood. We exchanged a few quiet words among ourselves, hoping he would finish his calls and that the journey could settle down. I was further annoyed because I felt this was adding to the inconvenience Sir and Ma'am had already experienced after our car broke down.

Whenever we travel with Sir and Ma'am, we try to be quiet, listen and learn from them as they converse, or simply give them their own space. This time, however, we found ourselves listening to something quite different: loud, harsh and angry words being spoken over the phone.

Gradually, Anand's calls ended, and we continued our journey. The air began to grow cooler and more pleasant, and Sir and Ma'am started discussing something about the event. Their calm demeanour, gentle voices and peaceful temperament brought a noticeable shift in the atmosphere inside the car.

It seemed as though Anand, too, was affected by this. Eventually, he turned towards Dr. Newton and asked, "What do you do, sir?" Sir simply replied, "I teach meditation."

And that was how the conversation began.

I was amazed by how the great teachers and masters can remain so humble and how, time and again, they manage to stay connected to their very core. Yet, I couldn't hold back and found myself telling Anand that Sir and Ma'am were the founders of a university in Vikarabad, were great teachers and had travelled to more than 120 countries. I wanted him to know how fortunate we were to be travelling with them and that they were on their way to speak at the very event we were attending.

The word meditation somehow became the link between Anand and us. He told us that one of his cousins was a meditator and an active member of the Pyramid Society. That cousin had been urging Anand to practise meditation, but Anand had never had the opportunity to do so. On learning this, we invited Anand to attend the event, have food and then leave. It was almost noon, and he would be able to listen to Sir and Ma'am's lecture and have lunch before leaving. He initially hesitated but ultimately agreed to stay on.

When Compassion Finds a Personal Meaning

The Ahimsa Dhyana Yagnam focused on vegetarianism and non-violence. During the programme, Dr. Newton and Dr. Lakshmi conducted a session that invited participants to look at compassion in a deeply personal way. The lecture was amazing and mesmerising, as usual, and towards the end, Ma'am guided everyone through a reflection.

She asked everyone to place their hands on their heart and feel all the animals as their own children. She invited them to consider that an animal's young is just as precious to its mother as our children are to us. The love a parent feels, the instinct to protect, and the pain of imagining its child suffering are not feelings that belong to human beings alone.

After the session concluded, we found Anand standing outside, waiting for us. He insisted on dropping us back at the place where he had picked us up. Then, he spoke his heart out. He said, "I heard your class, and I am very touched. I also have a very young child, about three years old. And you said that animals are like our children, and they are equally important to their mothers and fathers, their family. I could feel how I would feel if my child had pain. And from now on, I will become a vegetarian, and I will also meditate. From today, I will no longer have any non-vegetarian food."

His heartfelt words brought an instant smile to our faces. A person who had joined us almost by chance had found something that resonated with him so deeply that he wanted to make a change in his own life.

The Higher Plan

Sir and Ma'am rejoiced and appreciated his decision to turn vegetarian. Throughout the journey, they kept telling him how happy they were.

And then Sir said to me, "Harika, now you know? I told you there's a bigger plan, there's a higher plan. The universe always does things for a reason. This is the higher plan. He had to turn vegetarian today. That's why the car did not start, and we had to come by cab. And why only this cab? Why only him?"

His words brought me back to the moment when the car had refused to start. We had been so caught up in the inconvenience, the delay and our responsibility to get Sir and Ma'am to the venue on time that all we could see was a problem that needed to be solved. We had no way of knowing that this unexpected cab ride would bring Anand into an environment where he could listen, reflect and discover something for himself.

No one had planned this encounter or set out that morning to change his mind. It had unfolded through an ordinary conversation, an invitation to attend a session and a reflection that connected with his own experience of love as a parent.

I keep thinking about Anand and the choice he made that day. One person's decision to embrace vegetarianism can mean many lives spared. And when that choice becomes part of a family's way of life, its influence may reach well beyond one individual.

We had set out that morning to take Sir and Ma'am to Hyderabad for a programme on ahimsa. Somewhere along the way, the journey became an experience of ahimsa itself, one that reached a stranger and gave him a reason to look at life differently.

The car had refused to start. But something meaningful had begun.
